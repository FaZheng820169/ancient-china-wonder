The mortal realm has a **second population**, mostly invisible, and its census categories matter. Confuse them and a story loses its logic, because the three categories obey three different sets of rules.

## 1. Jing and guai: things that have woken up

**Jing (精)** and **guai (怪)** — usually lumped together as *yaoguai* — are things that have **woken up**. The rule is simple and immense: anything that lives or lasts long enough, or absorbs enough essence of sun and moon, can become animate and intelligent.

A fox of a hundred years becomes a fox-spirit. An ancient tree learns to speak. A rock, a lute, a mirror, a broom — given enough centuries and the right influences — may grow a soul. This is why the white peony in Pu Songling's tale can love a scholar, and why the first question to ask of any marvel is: **how old is it?**

Their morality is not fixed. A spirit may be grateful, predatory, mischievous, or simply strange — living its own life until a human blunders into it. What the genre insists on is that they are ***beings***, not evil incarnate: most can be reasoned with, many can be loved, and nearly all are bound by rules a clever human can learn. [The Monster Feared Reason](/stories/monster-feared-reason) is the category's emblem: the thing is defeated by an argument.

## 2. Yishou: the strange beasts as species

**Yishou (异兽)** are a different matter — not awakened objects but **species**: the fauna of the *Shan Hai Jing*, the Classic of Mountains and Seas. This is China's bestiary, thousands of entries deep:

- the **nine-tailed fox** (see [Fox Spirits](/worldview/fox-spirits))
- the **taotie**, a glutton-mask that is all appetite
- the **qilin**, a chimera of supreme gentleness that appears at the birth of sages
- the **baize**, which knows the name and weakness of every spirit under heaven
- birds with nine heads, fish that fly, oxen with a single leg

Unlike the sprites, these are simply ***there*** — native creatures of a stranger, larger world, catalogued by the old geographers with the sober tone of field notes. They are not villains. They are **wildlife**.

## 3. The category error to avoid

[Dragons](/worldview/dragons) deserve their own entry, but note the difference: the dragon is **neither sprite nor beast** — it is a water-*god*, a member of the divine administration with jurisdiction over rain, rivers and seas.

Three categories, three rules:

| Category | Origin | Morality | Defeated by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Jing / guai** (sprites) | woke up through age | variable | its own rule |
| **Yishou** (beasts) | always was | animal | hazard, traps, knowledge |
| **Shen / xian** (gods) | appointed or cultivated | official | jurisdiction, audit |

And **ghosts** are a fourth thing again: humans with unfinished status. See [Ghosts](/worldview/ghosts).

## 4. Seniority is power

The general principle unifying all of it is the one the whole universe runs on: **seniority is power**. Age, in this cosmos, is not decay but accumulation — of essence, of wisdom, of the ability to change shape and work wonders.

The oldest things in the room are always the most dangerous, and the most interesting. [When the Stones Spoke](/stories/when-the-stones-spoke) and [The Eye on Its Back](/stories/the-eye-on-its-back) both build a whole plot on this single premise.

## 5. Hard rules for writing it

1. **Categorise before you describe.** Sprite, beast, god, or ghost — the rules differ, and the reader will notice.
2. **Give it an age.** If you cannot say how long it has been accumulating, the being has no physics.
3. **Sprites are beings, not scenery.** Even the monstrous one gets an interior logic.
4. **Beasts are not villains.** Treat them as fauna with habits, and the horror lands harder.
5. **Let the clever human win.** The tradition's favourite weapon is not a sword but a correctly identified rule.
