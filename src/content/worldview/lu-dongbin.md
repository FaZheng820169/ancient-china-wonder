Of all the immortals, the Chinese have loved **Lü Dongbin (吕洞宾)** the longest and the most warmly — and the foundation myth of his cult is, essentially, the tale of *The World Inside a Pillow*.

He is the immortal who was made by a **failed examination**, and that is why he has always been everyone's favourite.

## 1. The Handan dream

The legend says he was Lü Yán, a scholar of the late Tang, travelling to the capital to sit the examinations. On the road through **Handan** he stopped at an inn and fell in with a Taoist — Zhongli Quan, himself an immortal in disguise.

While the millet cooked, the young scholar dozed on the Taoist's pillow and dreamed his whole life: examinations passed, high office won, marriage into a great family, fifty years of glory and intrigue and slander and exile, down to a deathbed surrounded by mourning sons. He woke with **the millet still uncooked**, and understood, and abandoned the examinations on the spot to follow the Taoist into the mountains.

This is the single most famous dream in Chinese literature, and its argument is precise: not that life is *sad*, but that it is *too small to be worth the ambition*. Fifty years fitted inside a cooking time.

## 2. The ten trials

There he was tested, and the tradition loves its **ten trials** — temptations of fear, lust, wealth and pity, all of which he passed. Then he was given the arts: the inner alchemy, and a sword.

The trials matter structurally: they convert [cultivation](/worldview/cultivation-system) from a schedule into a **character test**. Anyone can endure fifty years of breathing exercises; almost nobody passes the one where a beautiful woman walks into the room. See [Lü Dongbin Tests a Hermit](/stories/lu-dongbin-tests-a-hermit) for the test administered in the other direction.

## 3. The sword of wisdom

His sword is the famous **sword of wisdom**, which does not kill men but cuts away delusion — though in the martial tales it kills demons perfectly well, and in [The Swordsman Who Came for the Judge](/stories/the-swordsman-who-came-for-the-judge) it does its work on the wicked without ceremony.

The double function is the point: the same blade clears the mind and clears the road. That is exactly how a Taoist immortal's power is supposed to look — not overwhelming force but **discrimination**, applied lethally.

## 4. The wanderer who tests

He became the great wandering immortal: riding the clouds with sword and gourd, appearing as a beggar, a boatman, a drunkard in wineshops, **testing the hearts of strangers** and rewarding the few who pass.

This is his genre contribution. He turns up disguised, does something outrageous, and finds out who you are. [Du Zi Chun](/stories/du-zi-chun) and [The Bone of Arrogance](/stories/the-bone-of-arrogance) both run on this pattern, and it has been borrowed by every wandering-master story since.

## 5. White Peony, and the problem with him

He three times tried to enlighten the courtesan White Peony — and the tales about it are frankly disreputable, which is part of his charm. He is credited with poems, swordsmanship, medicine and a magnificent temper; he helps or teases half the figures of later mythology.

He is not a saint. He is the immortal who behaves like a **clever drunk uncle with supernatural powers**, and the tradition has always preferred him for it.

## 6. Patron of the failed

Temples to him spread across China, and he became the patron of barbers, ink-makers, scholars and the down-and-out — everyone, in short, who had ever **failed an examination**, in any sense, and needed a god who understood.

That is the real source of his popularity. He is the one high figure in the pantheon whose entire mythology is built out of a *lost* career.

## 7. Hard rules for writing him

1. **He is the counter to the Career.** His whole point is that the exam was not worth passing.
2. **Disguise first, wonder second.** He arrives as a beggar or a drunkard; the power is revealed late.
3. **The test is the plot.** If Lü is present, someone is being examined.
4. **Keep him slightly improper.** The moment he becomes dignified, he stops being Lü Dongbin.
5. **The sword cuts delusion.** Use it on what a character believes, not only on what attacks them.
