Western readers tend to translate both words as 'god', and miss the distinction on which half the mythology runs. In this universe, a **shen (神)** and a **xian (仙)** are different kinds of being, arrived at by opposite roads.

One is **hired**. The other is **self-taught**. Nearly every confusion a reader has about the Chinese supernatural resolves once that sentence is in place.

## 1. Gods are appointed

The celestial world is a bureaucracy, and a god is a **civil servant of heaven**: the God of Thunder, the City God of a walled town, the Dragon King of a lake, the Kitchen God who reports each household to heaven once a year.

Crucially, many gods were **once humans**. An exceptionally virtuous or loyal official is, after death, *appointed* to a post in the invisible administration — exactly as the emperor appoints magistrates in the visible one. Gods have jurisdiction, duties, and performance reviews. They can be promoted and, in the stories, demoted, sued and outwitted. [The Perfected Man of Fuling](/stories/the-perfected-man-of-fuling) and [The Gods Know No Textual Criticism](/stories/the-gods-know-no-textual-criticism) both turn on officials who are found to be less than their credentials claim.

This is why gods need **offerings**: a post without revenue is a post without authority.

## 2. Immortals are self-made

A xian is a being — human, usually — who has escaped death not by appointment but by **[cultivation](/worldview/cultivation-system)**: centuries of meditation, breathing arts, alchemy, diet, sexual discipline, or the simple refusal of worldly attachment, until the body is refined into something that no longer ages.

Immortals hold **no office**. They live on mountains and islands, ride cranes, turn up as ragged beggars and drunken swordsmen, and delight in ignoring the hierarchy the gods maintain. Where the god is the establishment, the immortal is the dropout who beat the system.

## 3. The tension: the office and the mountain

The two classes eye each other warily, and heaven periodically tries to recruit famous immortals into office. The Jade Emperor offers posts; the freer immortals decline. The greatest of them — the **Eight Immortals** — become folk-heroes precisely because they answer to no one: [Lü Dongbin](/worldview/lu-dongbin) with his demon-slaying sword, Iron-crutch Li with his gourd of medicine, drunken, ragged, magnificent.

The tension between the office and the mountain is one of the oldest arguments in Chinese civilisation, wearing a mythic costume: **the career and the drop-out, the appointment and the achievement, the state's blessing and the individual's escape.**

## 4. How to tell them apart

For the story-reader, the distinction is a working compass:

- If a supernatural figure **behaves like a magistrate** — holds court, keeps ledgers, demands offerings, enforces jurisdiction, answers to a superior — it is a **god**.
- If it **behaves like a wandering eccentric** with absurd powers, no visible job, and a habit of showing up disguised to test people — it is an **immortal**.
- If it is a **virtuous dead person promoted to a post**, it is a god, and its cult will have a shrine.
- If it is a **beast or object that grew into power**, it is neither — see [Sprites and Monsters](/worldview/monsters-and-yaoguai).

## 5. Hard rules for writing it

1. **Decide the road before the powers.** Gods are appointed; immortals are self-made. Get that right and the behaviour follows.
2. **Gods need temples, immortals need stories.** A god's authority shows in cult; an immortal's shows in anecdote.
3. **Let gods be audited.** Their jurisdiction is the plot; their accountability is the twist.
4. **Immortals should be rude.** Their defining trait is that the hierarchy does not impress them.
5. **Never let one class do the other's job.** A god who wanders is no longer a god; an immortal who collects tithes has become one.
