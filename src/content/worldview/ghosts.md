A **ghost (鬼, *gui*)** in this universe is not a different species from a human. It is a human in the wrong state: a soul that, for some reason, did not get processed through the Courts of the Dead, and so lingers in the mortal realm — off the register, out of the queue, stuck.

The reasons are remarkably consistent across two thousand years of stories:

- **A violent or untimely death.** Murdered, drowned, executed unjustly — the dead who were wronged stay to see the wrong addressed.
- **No burial, or a botched one.** The unburied cannot be processed. An enormous number of ghost stories are resolved by nothing more supernatural than *finding the bones and burying them properly*.
- **An unsettled debt or vow.** A promise unkept, a wrong unavenged, a love cut off — the soul refuses to drink the soup of forgetfulness while the ledger is open.
- **Excess of feeling.** Grief, love or hatred strong enough to anchor a soul against the current of rebirth.

Ghosts in zhiguai run on **rules**, and the rules are the plot. They fear the things of strong living essence: human spit, roosters' cries, sunlight, peach-wood, dog's blood, mirrors, thunder, and above all the seals and swords of Taoist exorcists. They are bound by the terms of their own condition — a drowned ghost must drown another to be released; a ghost under a demon's command, like Nie Xiaoqian, cannot simply refuse. Learn a ghost's rule and you have the story.

And the tradition's deepest instinct about ghosts is not fear but *administration*. Most ghosts want something specific and finishable: burial, justice, a message delivered, a debt paid. A ghost with its business settled goes quietly. This is why the Chinese ghost story is so often, at bottom, a detective story — the haunting is a case file, and the hero's job is to close it.

The exceptions are the predators: the painted-skin demons that wear humanity as bait, the old corpses that rise to drink blood. But even these are usually working a scheme, and a scheme, once understood, can be broken.
