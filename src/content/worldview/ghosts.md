A **ghost (鬼, *gui*)** in this universe is not a different species from a human. It is a **human in the wrong state**: a soul that, for some reason, was never processed through the [Courts of the Dead](/worldview/underworld), and so lingers in the mortal realm — off the register, out of the queue, stuck.

That is the tradition's defining move, and it is worth sitting with. The Chinese ghost is not a demon, not a fallen thing, not a creature of another order. It is a person with an unresolved administrative status. Everything else about it follows from that.

## 1. Four causes — and why each one is a plot

The reasons a soul fails to move on are remarkably consistent across two thousand years of stories, and each one generates a different kind of tale.

- **A violent or untimely death.** Murdered, drowned, executed unjustly — the dead who were wronged stay to see the wrong addressed. This is the revenge plot, and it is why so many ghosts are also plaintiffs: see [The Girl Ghost Who Filed Suits](/stories/the-girl-ghost-who-filed-suits) and [The Chaste Girl Who Cried Injustice](/stories/the-chaste-girl-who-cried-injustice).
- **No burial, or a botched one.** The unburied cannot be processed. An enormous number of ghost stories are resolved by nothing more supernatural than *finding the bones and burying them properly* — the horror is a housing problem.
- **An unsettled debt or vow.** A promise unkept, a wrong unavenged, a love cut off. The soul refuses the broth of forgetfulness while the ledger is open. This is why a haunting so often turns out to be about money, marriage, or a signature.
- **Excess of feeling.** Grief, love or hatred strong enough to anchor a soul against the current of rebirth. [Two Tales of Jealous Ghosts](/stories/two-tales-of-jealous-ghosts) and [The Daughter of Zhang Ten Thousand](/stories/the-daughter-of-zhang-ten-thousand) run on this engine.

Four causes, four plots: **injustice, burial, debt, love**. Almost every ghost story in this library is one of those four wearing a different costume.

## 2. What they fear: the rulebook

Ghosts run on **rules**, and the rules are the plot. They are creatures of *yin* — shadow, cold, night — and they are undone by concentrated *yang*: living essence in its strong forms.

The standard apotropaics recur so reliably that they function as a shared grammar: **human spit**, **the crowing of a rooster**, **sunlight**, **peach-wood**, **dog's blood**, **mirrors**, **thunder**, and above all the **seals, swords and water-rites of a Taoist exorcist**. A ghost that can shrug off a talisman is making a statement; a ghost that flees from a rooster is simply being a ghost.

They are also bound by the terms of their own condition. A **drowned ghost must find a substitute** — it cannot move on until another body takes its place in the water, which turns every riverside in the tradition into a hunting ground and every drowning into a possible sequel. A ghost under a demon's command, like [Nie Xiaoqian](/stories/nie-xiaoqian), cannot simply refuse; her coercion *is* her character. Learn a ghost's specific rule and you have the story.

## 3. What they want: the haunting as case file

The tradition's deepest instinct about ghosts is not fear but **administration**. Most ghosts want something specific and *finishable*: burial, justice, a message delivered, a debt paid, a son bought. A ghost with its business settled goes quietly.

This is why the Chinese ghost story is so often, at bottom, a **detective story**. The haunting is a case file. The hero's job is not to destroy the ghost but to work out what it is owed and close the case — as in [The Ghost Who Bought a Son](/stories/the-ghost-who-bought-a-son), [The Ghost Who Brought Dumplings](/stories/the-ghost-who-brought-dumplings), or [Summoned as a Witness](/stories/summoned-as-a-witness), where the dead are simply needed to testify.

Note the tonal consequence: violence is usually the *wrong* answer. A ghost you fight is a ghost you have misread.

## 4. A short taxonomy

The tradition sorts its dead more finely than English allows:

- **吊死鬼** — the hanged ghost, often with the rope still on it, and a tongue that tells you the manner of death
- **水鬼 / 溺鬼** — the drowned one, hunting a substitute
- **产鬼** — the ghost of a woman who died in childbirth, bound to the blood and the room
- **饿鬼** — the hungry ghost, throat narrow as a needle, eternally unable to swallow
- **厉鬼** — the vengeful fierce ghost, the one that will not be reasoned with
- **僵尸** — not really a ghost at all, but the reanimated corpse; see [The Corpse That Does Not Become a Ghost](/worldview/the-corpse-that-does-not-become-a-ghost) for why the distinction matters
- **鬼吏 / 差人** — ghost *officials*, the bailiffs who come to collect the living on schedule

The last category is the tell. In this cosmos even the dead have a civil service.

## 5. The predators, and why they are rarer than you expect

The exceptions are the hunters: the painted-skin demons that wear humanity as bait, the old corpses that rise to drink blood, the things in [Boiling the Ghost in Oil](/stories/boiling-the-ghost-in-oil) and [The Coffin Lid That Flew](/stories/the-coffin-lid-that-flew). But even these are usually **working a scheme**, and a scheme, once understood, can be broken.

What is striking is how *few* of them there are. The overwhelming majority of ghosts in these stories are not monsters. They are people with a problem — and the problem is almost always that someone, living or dead, did not do their job.

## 6. Hard rules for writing ghosts

1. **Start from the cause, not the scare.** Pick one of the four causes; the haunting's shape follows from it.
2. **Give it a finishable want.** A ghost that wants something vague is a ghost the reader cannot root for or solve.
3. **Violence is the wrong answer.** The satisfying resolution is usually paperwork, burial, or restitution.
4. **State its rule.** What it fears, what binds it, what releases it — and keep to it.
5. **The substitute rule is a trap and a gift.** If your ghost needs a replacement, every bystander is in danger, and the hero's dilemma is real.
6. **It is a person, not a species.** The moment you let the reader forget that, the story stops being a Chinese ghost story.
