A living person in this universe is a composite. Two souls inhabit one body, and they have different natures, different appetites, and different destinations.

**The hun (魂)** is the cloud-soul: *yang*, light, clear, intelligent. It is the part that thinks, remembers, loves, and composes poetry. It is associated with breath and with the liver. When a person dies, the hun leaves and goes upward.

**The po (魄)** is the white-soul: *yin*, heavy, clouded, dull. It is the part bound to the flesh — appetite, sensation, the body's sheer habit of continuing. It stays with the corpse.

The classical formula usually gives a person **three hun and seven po**, and the numbers matter less than the direction: the hun ascends, the po descends with the body into the earth.

## Why this explains the monsters

The single most useful thing about the doctrine is that it turns "walking corpse" from a horror trope into a diagnosis.

- **While the hun is present, it is the person.** A newly dead soul that comes back to settle its affairs — to ask for its mother to be fed, its essays printed, its debts paid — comes back *whole*: it weeps, it reasons, it says goodbye properly. This is the first half of 《南昌士人》.
- **Once the hun scatters, it is not the person.** What is left standing in the room is the po wearing a face: the shape without the mind, driven by the body's last appetites. It cannot be argued with, only outrun or mastered. This is the second half of the same story — the moment the dead man's eyes go blank and his face begins to spoil.

As Yuan Mei's commentator puts it: *魂在，则其人也；魂去，则非其人也* — while the hun is present it is the person; once the hun is gone it is not.

## What follows from it

**Possession and borrowed bodies.** Because the soul is not identical with the body, a soul can in principle occupy a *different* body. 借尸还魂 — "borrowing a corpse to return the soul" — is the standard mechanism, and it is why the resurrected in these stories so often wake in the wrong face, the wrong marriage, and the wrong village. See 《灵璧女借尸还魂》.

**Exchanging forms.** The same logic has a criminal use. In 《李通判》 a Daoist intends to move his own soul into a rich young man's body; the trick is caught because the shape emerging from the coffin is the master's while the *voice* is the priest's. Shape and person are separable, and the separation is a technology.

**Why the dead need burying.** A body with the po still in it is not inert. It can travel — hence the later Hunan legends of the corpse-drivers who marched the dead home over hundreds of miles, and hence the horror of an unburied or disturbed grave.

**Why ghosts are cold.** The po is yin. Where it concentrates, the temperature drops, and the drop is the first thing a living body notices. In 《骷髅吹气》 the victim measures his own haunting: a cold spot the size of a walnut, then a plate, then half the mat, then into the bone.

## The one who can master it

The texts are consistent: *惟有道之人为能制魄* — only a person of the Way can master the po. Not a magistrate, not a soldier, and not courage. It takes someone whose own hun is disciplined enough to out-face a soul that has none.
