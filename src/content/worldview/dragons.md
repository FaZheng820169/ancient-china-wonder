Forget the dragon of Western fantasy — the winged hoarder on a mountain of gold. The Chinese **long (龙)** is something else entirely: a **god of water**, and a working member of the celestial civil service.

That single reclassification explains almost every dragon story in this library. A dragon is not a monster to be killed. It is an **official with a jurisdiction**, and the jurisdiction is the water-cycle.

## 1. Every body of water has one

The Four Seas are ruled by four **Dragon Kings**, brothers, each holding court in a crystal palace at the bottom of his sea. Every great river and lake has its own — the Dragon King of Dongting, the Dragon of the Qiantang, and, in the humblest ditches, a local dragon no grander than the pond it lives in. The scale runs from ocean to puddle without a break in the chain of command.

Their remit is rain, flood, drought and tide. When a district needs rain it petitions the dragon exactly as it would petition a magistrate: offerings, processions, prayers — and in hard times, **threats**. More than one folk tradition includes beating the dragon-king's statue when the rain fails, which is the surest evidence that the people understood him as an official rather than a god.

## 2. Quotas, and dragons who are prosecuted

Dragons answer **upward**. Rainfall quotas are issued from heaven, and a dragon who rains too much, too little, or in a temper is not merely rude — he is *prosecuted*. Several stories open with a dragon-king beheaded or chained for flooding the wrong county.

This is the detail that separates the Chinese dragon from every other dragon in world mythology: **it is accountable**. It can err, be reported, be sentenced, and be replaced. In [The Four Generals and the River Dragon](/stories/the-four-generals-and-the-river-dragon) a long *jiao* has made a Yellow River dike unbuildable and is simply executed by a diver with a borrowed sword — after which the killer is enfeoffed as a god himself, which is very much how this cosmos handles a staffing problem.

## 3. The chimera body: the animal shape of weather

In form the long is the great composite of Chinese description — the horns of a stag, the head of a camel, the eyes of a demon, the neck of a snake, the belly of a clam, the scales of a fish, the claws of an eagle, the palms of a tiger, the ears of an ox. It can shrink to a worm or fill the sky; it flies without wings; it is the animal shape of **weather itself**.

Two related creatures are worth separating. The **jiao (蛟)** is a hornless river-dragon or flood-serpent — the thing that lives in a particular stretch of water and breaks the dykes. The **chi (螭)** is a lesser, decorative dragon. Common English flattens all three into "dragon," but the stories distinguish them by habitat and temperament, and so should you.

## 4. The court below the water

Dragon courts mirror the human one, and the stories exploit the resemblance for everything it is worth: ministers, armies of **shrimp-soldiers and crab-generals**, marriage alliances, family feuds, and domestic disasters conducted at the scale of typhoons. [Liu Yi](/stories/liu-yi) turns on a dragon princess abused by her dragon husband, whose rescue is at once a family intervention and a natural catastrophe.

Because the court is a court, dragon stories are frequently **bureaucratic comedies** — and occasionally petitions from below. In [The Water People of Dingtao](/stories/the-water-people-of-dingtao) a green-robed envoy appears to a magistrate in a dream and pleads, in the language of a subject asking for protection, that his kind are being netted and eaten; when the ban lapses, the river answers with a monster six or seven zhang long. The dragon kingdom here is not a threat. It is a **constituency**.

## 5. Dragons and people: the three standard transactions

Almost every human–dragon story is one of three deals:

- **Petition** — you ask for rain and pay for it in offerings, processions, or a temple.
- **Marriage** — a dragon takes or is given a human spouse, and the family acquires weather. [The Wife Taken by the Lake Dragon](/stories/the-wife-taken-by-the-lake-dragon) shows the darker version: a pregnancy is claimed from a locked room, and the village explains it as a dragon who came to the woman in a dream.
- **Trespass** — you harm the water and the water harms back. In [The Flood That Rose From the Well](/stories/the-flood-that-rose-from-the-well) jets break through a courtyard floor, and the experts conclude the flood-dragons "rode the water's force" — the disaster is not an accident but a will using the storm as a horse.

## 6. The emperor's emblem, and the older dragon

Politically the dragon became the emblem of the emperor — the Son of Heaven as the land's counterpart to the water-lords. But the story-tradition kept the older, earthier creature: powerful, official, occasionally foolish, sometimes petulant, and always, at bottom, **answerable** — like everything else in this universe — to the ledger of consequences.

## 7. Hard rules for writing dragons

1. **It is an official.** Give it a jurisdiction, a superior, and a quota. A dragon without a remit is just a lizard.
2. **Distinguish long, jiao and chi.** Habitat and rank first, spectacle second.
3. **It can be prosecuted.** The most interesting dragon stories are the ones where the dragon is in the wrong.
4. **Water is the relationship.** Any human–dragon encounter is really about rain, flood, fishing, or a boundary crossing.
5. **The court is comic potential.** Shrimp-soldiers and crab-generals are not decoration; they are a satire of every yamen in the empire.
