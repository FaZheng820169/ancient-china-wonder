In most Western supernatural fiction, a ghost is a threat. It has power, you have none, and the story is about whether you survive it.

Chinese zhiguai do not work that way. A Chinese ghost has a **move list**, the move list is short, and — this is the part that surprises people — the texts state explicitly what happens when the list runs out.

## Three tricks, and then it is finished

The doctrine is the title of a tale in 《子不語》: 《鬼有三技過此鬼道乃窮》 — *the ghost has three tricks; get past them and the ghost's road comes to an end.*

A scholar named Lü, who styles himself Mr Open-Minded, is walking home at night. A woman steps out of a tree and blocks him. He goes left, she blocks left; he goes right, she blocks right. He recognises the thing everyone calls 鬼打牆 — the ghost wall — and walks straight at her.

She changes. Hair loose, blood running, tongue out a foot, hopping at him. And he says:

> 汝前之塗眉畫粉，迷我也；向前阻拒，遮我也；今作此惡狀，嚇我也。三技畢矣，我總不怕，想無他技可施。
>
> First the painted brows and powder — that was to bewitch me. Then standing in my way — that was to block me. Now this hideous shape — that is to frighten me. The three tricks are used up. I am simply not afraid. I take it you have nothing else.

The three tricks are **bewitchment, obstruction, terror**. That is the complete set. A ghost that has tried all three on someone who did not flinch has no fourth move.

## It cannot hurt you unless you cooperate

《蔡書生》 is one hundred and twenty characters long and contains the whole mechanism.

A scholar buys a haunted house nobody will live in. He sits up with a candle. At midnight a woman comes in, a red silk at her neck, bows to him, ties a noose to the beam, and puts her head into it. Then she hangs a second rope and beckons him.

Cai puts one foot into the second noose.

The woman says: *You have made a mistake.*

Cai laughs and says: *You made the mistake — that is why you are what you are today. I am not making one.*

> 汝誤才有今日，我勿誤也。

The ghost wails, prostrates herself twice, and leaves. The haunting ends. Cai goes on to pass the examination.

The exchange turns entirely on one word — 誤, *mistake* — but look at what the ghost needed. She did not attack him. She **invited him to step into the rope**, and the whole horror depends on his doing it. He puts in a foot and refuses the rest, and the mechanism has nothing to grip.

## Reason is a weapon, but not the way you expect

In 《怪怕講理》 an old man of eighty is visited by something. He does not run or chant. He says:

> 足下是鬼耶，此來甚差！我年已八十餘，死乃旦夕事，不久與君為同類，何必先蒙過訪？若是仙耶，何不請坐一談？
>
> Are you a ghost, sir? Then you have come on a very foolish errand. I am past eighty; dying is a matter of morning or evening for me, and soon I shall be the same kind of thing as you. Why trouble to call in first? Or if you are an immortal — why not sit down and talk?

The thing does not answer. It howls, and every window in the building flies open with a rush of cold air.

Yuan Mei's title claims the monster fears reasoning. What actually happens is more interesting: the monster cannot **answer** reasoning. It has no reply to a man who has already agreed to die. The howl and the wind are what is left when an argument cannot be met.

## Breath beats weapons

《治鬼二妙》 — *two excellent ways to govern a ghost* — is not a story at all. It is two sentences of doctrine from two different men.

The Daoist Lou: when you meet a ghost, do not be afraid; **blow your breath at it**. Use the formless against the formless. 鬼最畏氣，轉勝刀棍也 — what a ghost most fears is breath; it works better than blade or cudgel.

The scholar Zhang Qishi: when you see a ghost, do not be afraid. *Fight it. If you win, good. If you lose — I shall simply be the same as it.*

Both are instructions about your own state, not about equipment. The second is pure logic: the worst outcome is the one already waiting for you, so the threat is empty.

## One character, written with two fingers

In 《縊鬼畏魄字》 the counter is startlingly specific. A hanging ghost is chasing someone, and the person who knows the method says:

> 但以左手兩指寫一「魄」字，指之入地，彼一入，不能出矣。
>
> Only write the character 魄 with two fingers of the left hand, point it into the ground, and once it has gone in it cannot come out.

Not a talisman on paper. Not a chant. A single written character, drawn in the air with a particular hand, and the physics of it is literal: the ghost enters the character and is held there.

Note what this implies about the universe. Writing is not a description of the world. **Writing is an instruction the world obeys.**

## How death actually arrives

Not every ghost wants to bewitch you. 《一目五先生》 describes five plague ghosts in Zhejiang: four are entirely blind, one has a single eye, and the four depend on him to see. In plague years they walk together and **smell sleepers**.

> 一鬼嗅則其人病，五鬼共嗅則其人死。

One ghost smelling you makes you ill. All five smelling you kills you.

The mechanism is dose, not malice. There is no judgement in it, no chosen victim — there is a quantity of exposure and a threshold. And the only ghost with eyes is the one giving orders; the other four shuffle along behind, unable to act on their own.

## The failure mode: wanting it too much

The funniest tale in this group is also the one that closes the system.

《鬼避姜三莽》: Jiang Sanmang of Jingcheng hears the story of Song Dingbo, who caught a ghost, spat on it to turn it into a sheep, and sold it at the butcher's for a good price. Jiang is delighted. *So ghosts can be tied up. Tie one a night, spit it into a sheep, sell it in the morning, and that is a day's wine and meat paid for.*

Every night he takes a cudgel and a rope and creeps among the graves, 如獵者之伺狐兔 — like a hunter watching for fox and hare. He never meets one. He lies down pretending to be drunk at places famous for hauntings. Nothing comes. One night he sees will-o'-the-wisps across the trees, charges at them, and they scatter before he arrives.

Finally, from the edge of the wood, one ghost laughs:

> 吾等今日真怯矣！
>
> We have been real cowards today!

Jiang Sanmang cannot meet a ghost **because he wants to**. The thing that makes ghosts dangerous — fear — is precisely what he lacks, and his lack of it is not courage but appetite. The universe has a balancing valve: the man who is immune is also the man who cannot collect.

## The move list, assembled

| A ghost can | A ghost cannot |
| --- | --- |
| Change its shape and bewitch | Hurt someone who refuses to cooperate |
| Block your path | Answer an argument it cannot refute |
| Show a terrifying form | Act once its three tricks have failed |
| Smell you into illness | Appear to a man who hopes to catch one |
| Be deceived, bound and sold | Survive being written into the ground |

The counters are as fixed as the attacks: **breath, one written character, an argument, a refusal to step into the rope, and a willingness to lose.**

For a reader coming from Western horror, the striking thing is that none of this requires power. Mr Open-Minded has no amulet. Cai has no scripture. The eighty-year-old man has no weapon at all. They win by **responding correctly** — and the stories are unusually candid that responding correctly is a matter of temperament, not virtue.

That is why so many of the survivors are eccentrics: a man who calls himself Open-Minded, a scholar who argues about a pun while standing in a noose, a fool with a cudgel. In this universe, the way to beat a ghost is not to be holier than it.

It is to be stranger than it.
