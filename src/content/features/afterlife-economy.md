When a Chinese family burns paper money at a grave, it is easy to file the custom under piety. The dead are honoured; the living feel better.

But read a few hundred zhiguai tales side by side and a different picture assembles itself. The stories agree with each other on a remarkable number of specifics: what is owed, who collects it, what the exchange rate is, and what happens when the account will not balance. The agreement is too consistent to be decoration.

**The Chinese underworld is not primarily a court of morality. It is a clearing house.** And it keeps immaculate books.

## There is a legal profit margin

The single most revealing office in the whole underworld is held by the 掠剩鬼, the Ghost Who Seizes Surplus. A dead merchant buys his living friend lunch and explains his new appointment:

> 凡吏人贾贩利息皆有数，过常数得之即为馀剩，吾得掠而有之。今人間如吾輩甚多。
>
> For every clerk and every trader there is a set figure of profit. Earn beyond the fixed number and the excess is surplus — and I am licensed to seize it. There are very many like me in the world of the living now.

This is not a demon. It is a tax inspector with a quota, and the quota is not his to set. Every human being has a lawful profit ceiling, and exceeding it is not a sin so much as an **overdraft that will be called in**.

The line also explains one of the most reliable plots in Chinese fiction: why the suddenly wealthy man always comes to grief. He did not become wicked. He went over his number.

## Debts survive death, and they are settled in kind

In 《大樂上人》 a poor yamen runner borrows seven ounces of silver from a monk over several years and can never repay it. He keeps saying: *I cannot repay your kindness. Let me be your donkey or horse in the next life and repay it that way.*

That night the monk's donkey foals. A buyer arrives, mounts it, and laughs: *I have cheated the monk. I love this donkey and I may not bring it back.* He rides off. On the table he has left seven ounces of white silver — exactly the sum owed.

In 《許元惠》 a man dreams of a guest in black who says: *Yesterday I borrowed three hundred cash from you. I have come to repay it.* Neither the lender nor the date can be recalled. The next day a black duck appears among the flock, lays one egg a day for a month, thirty eggs in all, and then stops. Thirty eggs come to exactly three hundred cash.

Note what these two tales have in common. The debt is **not forgiven by death, not forgotten, and not rounded off**. It is settled in whatever currency is available, at a rate that comes out even to the last coin. A soul is worth seven ounces of silver. An egg is worth ten cash. Nobody in these stories argues about the valuation.

## Debts are inherited

The harshest rule is also the one the texts state most plainly. 《香粉地獄》 describes a district of the underworld where the daughters and young wives of corrupt officials work as prostitutes, and gives the reasoning in one sentence:

> 陽世官宰犯貪酷二字敗露者，遭國法；稍或漏網，冥府錄其幼媳愛女，入青樓以償孽債。
>
> When an official in the world of the living is exposed for greed and cruelty, the law of the land deals with him. If he slips through the net, the courts of the dead enrol his young daughters-in-law and beloved daughters into the brothels, to pay off the debt of his evil.

Shen Qifeng then does the thing that makes it unbearable: he puts the narrator's own betrothed in the room. She is released only when her father donates eight hundred in gold to found six charity schools.

Two things follow. The liability is **transferable across persons**, and it is **redeemable at a published price**. This is not vengeance. It is a lien.

## Even gods must pay to take office

Being appointed a god does not exempt you from the running costs of the system. In 《曾尚書》 a dead minister receives a celestial writ making him Earth God of a mountain shrine, and his first act is to write to his sons:

> 方交承之始，闔府官僚當有私覿禮，不可廢。
>
> This is the moment of handing over and taking up. The whole body of officials in the yamen is owed the private gift of greeting. It cannot be dispensed with.

He instructs them to take the several thousand sheets of good paper in the east study, dye them yellow, print them with the hidden words of the Great Brahma, and burn them reverently.

They do not do it. He comes back, has his youngest son thrown down in front of a crowd at the temple, strips his clothes and beats him — and everyone present sees it.

A god is an office-holder, and office-holders have expenses. The currency is paper, and the paper has to be *prepared correctly*: dyed, printed, burned. Sloppy paperwork in this universe is not inefficiency. It is a punishable failure.

## Hell has a currency, and face is an asset

In 《鬼借官銜嫁女》 a woman commissions gold-foil hairpins from a boy, paying in cakes and cash. The next morning the cakes are clods of earth and the money is paper money. She had been buying her daughter's dowry.

She then asks the boy to obtain a sheet bearing a neighbour's official rank — not for any power it carries, but because she wants the lanterns at the wedding to look right. Yuan Mei's closing line: *so ghosts too love prestige and reverence power, just as the living do.*

Two separate facts here. The underworld has **its own money**, and it converts back into earth and paper on contact with daylight. And **prestige is itself a tradeable commodity**: a borrowed title has real value to a ghost household, and it can be acquired.

## Estates are held in trust

In 《女鬼守財待婿》 a wife is accused of stealing the household silver and giving it to a lover. She hangs herself. Her ghost then haunts the house, advancing one inch as the moonlight moves one inch, until her son-in-law finally identifies himself — at which point she releases him, explains why she died, and tells him where the silver is buried.

The property did not vanish, was not confiscated, and did not pass to the accusers. It stayed in the custody of the dead woman until the lawful heir turned up and named himself.

## Why it is accounts and not fire

Put these six together and the shape is consistent: a profit ceiling with a licensed collector, debts that survive death and settle at exact rates, liabilities that transfer to your children, appointment costs payable in correctly prepared paper, a native currency, and estates held in trust.

What is missing from that list is interesting. There is no fiery punishment anywhere in it. There is no moral verdict. Nobody is told they were wicked.

The underworld's characteristic act is not judgement. It is **reconciliation**. You can outrun the law of the land, and the tales are full of men who did. You cannot outrun the account — and the account will be settled against your daughter, or your next life as a donkey, or thirty eggs laid by a duck nobody in the family recognises.

For anyone building a story or a game in this universe, that is the useful thing. The most frightening figure in the Chinese afterlife is not a devil. It is an auditor who has been doing the job for a very long time, and who already knows your number.
