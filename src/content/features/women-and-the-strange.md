Read the Chinese strange tale expecting a Gothic heroine and you will be surprised by what you find. In the European tradition the woman in the ghost story is usually one of two things: the victim (the murdered bride, the wronged lady) or the haunter (the pale figure at the window). She is acted upon. The Chinese tradition, written by the same patriarchal society, repeatedly does something stranger. Its female figures are *agents*. They are scholars, deities, tricksters, guardians and judges — and, most unnervingly for a modern reader, the narrative voice is very often on their side.

This is not a feminist canon. The stories are still Confucian, still arranged around the family, still capable of ranking a woman by her face. But the *texture* is different, and the difference is the subject of this essay.

## 1. The girl who rewrote the classics

《筆頭減壽》 (The Girl Who Parodied the Classics) is the sharpest case. Zheng Lanfen is a girl of such ability that her mother calls her the family's *thousand-li colt* — except, she jokes, a mare and not a stallion. She then rewrites the *Book of Changes* as a hexagram on **Money**, and the *Songs of the Five Sons* as a mock rebuke to her maid. She is so good at it that her name is gossiped about through the county. Then she dreams into a heavenly hall where a crowned judge sentences the forgers of scripture — and discovers, to her terror, that she is on the docket too, for *altering the classics in play*. Her punishment is to have her lifespan cut.

Notice what the premise requires. A woman is imagined as a *plausible author of classical parody*. The cosmos does not punish her for being a woman; it punishes her for being a scholar who went too far. The story's wonder is that her intellect is never in question — only her impiety.

## 2. The goddess the women summoned

《紫姑神》 (The Goddess Zi Gu) turns a real folk cult into a love story. Zi Gu began, historically, as the spirit of a woman murdered in a latrine, and by the Tang she was a deity whom *women* consulted through a planchette to learn the future. Yuan Mei keeps the goddess but omits the latrine entirely; his Zi Gu is an immortal banished for eloping, who cannot climb back to Heaven and whom the underworld dares not file among the dead because of her rank. She is, in the story's own word, **unfileable** — neither damned nor saved — and she resolves her predicament by *petitioning* the Lord of Mount Tai to be entered in a register so she can be reborn. A female deity of women's divination, treated throughout as a subject with a bureaucratic problem of her own.

## 3. The marriage market, and who it wrongs

《桃夭村》 (Peach Blossom Village) is a satire so sharp it stings. A country marries its young people by **ranking the girls by their faces and the boys by their essays**, then matching first to first, last to last. A wealthy merchant bribes the examiner for first place and wins the bejewelled but hideous girl; an honest scholar refuses the bribe and is pushed to the bottom — where he lifts the veil and finds the beautiful poor girl, who had been demoted for *also* refusing the examiner. The ranking of women as goods is the joke's target, and the story's sympathy sits plainly with the wronged beauty and the man who kept his integrity. The system is exposed, not endorsed.

## 4. The temple that a mishearing built

《十姨廟》 (The Temple of the Ten Aunts) is perhaps the funniest indictment of the patriarchy in the whole collection. A temple near Duqu holds ten beautiful women. Nobody remembers it was built for **Du Fu**, whose office of *Reminder* (拾遺, *Shiyi*) sounded to country ears exactly like *Ten Aunts* (十姨, *Shiyi*). The aunts, far from being ashamed, write *jiju* — poems each line lifted from a different Tang poet and made to cohere, a difficult real exercise — and they out-argue a male college student at his own classical game. A great male poet's title becomes ten women, and they are funnier and more learned than the man sent to correct them. The punchline is the poet himself arriving and driving them out — but the laughter is not at the aunts.

## 5. The dead wife who ran the estate

Two stories make the same quiet point: the dead wife is the only competent person in the house. In 《女鬼守財待婿》 (The Ghost Who Guarded the Money), a wife is accused of giving the family silver to a lover, hangs herself in despair, and then haunts the house for years — not to avenge herself but to **guard the silver until her son-in-law arrives**. She nearly kills the very man she is waiting for, stopped only by his name, and then tells him where the money is buried. Yuan Mei arranges the plot so that the living husband's jealousy destroyed the household and the dead wife is its only competent manager.

In 《鬼婦持家》 (The Dead Wife Keeps House), a cruel second wife beats the children; the husband falls through the ground into the underworld and finds his dead first wife embroidering slippers at a window, not recognising him. The dead father sentences the living second wife to twelve years of instruction and **lends her body** to the dead first wife to run the house. The tale's anger is aimed, with unusual precision, at the husband — *never* at either wife.

## 6. And the famous cases

The iconic fox- and ghost-wives of the canon make the same move. Nie Xiaoqian is a ghost enslaved by an old demon who compels her to lure men to their deaths; she frees herself by warning the scholar Ning, helping him, and asking only to be reburied. Yingning is a fox girl whose constant laughter is not vacancy but a quiet subversion of every solemn household she enters. In both, the female figure is the one with agency, and the male lead is the one who must rise to her.

## What unites them

These are not liberated women by any modern measure. They are ranked, married off, expected to keep house and obey. But the genre grants them something the Gothic heroine is almost never granted: **the story treats them as subjects, and sides with them when they are wronged.** The girl is punished as a scholar; the goddess petitions as a citizen; the beautiful girl is the reward of integrity; the ten aunts outclass the man; the dead wife is exonerated by her own ghost. When you read a hundred of these tales, the pattern is hard to miss: the female strange is rarely the thing that happens *to* someone. She is the one the tale is *about* — and more often than not, the one it is rooting for.
