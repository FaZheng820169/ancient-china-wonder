In most Western fantasy, when a god wrongs you, the story becomes a fight. You defy, you steal fire, you get punished, and the shape of the tale is rebellion.

Chinese zhiguai keeps reaching for a different ending. Because the otherworld in these stories is not a mystery but a **court**, the natural response to a grievance is not defiance. It is a **petition**. And once you notice this, you see it everywhere: fathers suing on behalf of dead sons, ghosts filing written complaints at temple altars, villagers taking a hoe to a statue over breach of contract, and a god explaining in open court why he returned a false verdict on purpose.

This is the procedural side of the universe, and it is the part almost nobody outside China has read.

## The case that establishes the principle

《席方平》 is the founding document. A man's father dies claiming that a bully of a ghost bribed the officers of the dead to have him beaten. The son stops eating and says: *my father was a simple man, and now a bully of a ghost is beating him. I am going down there to argue his case myself.*

So he goes down — and the story is a lawsuit that climbs an entire administrative ladder. He petitions the **City God**, who has already been bought. He petitions the **magistrate of the dead**. He goes to the **prefecture**, and each office is worse than the last, until guards lay him on a bench and **saw him in two** for the crime of vexing his superiors. He wakes on the road with the wound healed and understands that they are trying to wear him out.

He is then laid on an iron bed with fire passed over him until his bones crack; when that fails, they take him apart and put him back together as a **baby**, and hand him to a noble family to be raised, and wait for him to forget.

He does not forget. At twenty he remembers, walks out of the house, goes back to the gate of hell and **files the same petition, in the same words, against the same people** — a case already refused eight times. This time a god's messenger passing on the road notices the man shouting outside the gate and carries the file up out of the underworld entirely, to **Er Lang**, nephew of the Jade Emperor, before whom no clerk can hide a document.

Er Lang tries them all in one session. Pu Songling writes out the judgement in full: the City God degraded to commoner, the judges flayed and demoted to the lowest grade of the celestial service, the bribes confiscated and returned, the clerks who sawed a man in half sent to the earth prisons for a fixed term — and to the father, restored to his body with a hundred years more of life, **compensation for every day of his beating**.

Damages, assessed per diem. From a god.

## Jurisdiction runs both ways

If the courts of the dead can be petitioned, it follows that the courts of the living can be petitioned **by the dead** — and the stories treat this as routine.

In 《田烈妻》 a magistrate holding an evening session under a bright moon sees a woman kneeling at his gate with her face covered in black cloth. Nobody improvises. He has his clerk hold up the writ and formally proclaim that a soul with a grievance may enter; the ghost drifts in and kneels at the correct spot below the steps. The court of the living is treated as having jurisdiction over the dead, and it runs on paperwork like everything else.

That is the deep structural joke of the whole universe: there is only **one** legal system, and it has two branches.

## How you actually file

Two instruments do most of the work, and both are real practices rather than inventions.

The first is **焚牒** — writing a plaint and burning it at the City God's temple. In 《城隍杀鬼不许为聻》 a family does exactly this about a rapist ghost, and the City God tries him, sentences him to forty strokes and a public cangue. (Yuan Mei keeps one detail: the clerk who serves the summons first asks for a fee, and quotes the exchange rate between paper money and underworld silver — the same corruption joke that fills 《席方平》, here in a single line.)

The second is **杯珓**, the pair of wooden crescents thrown before a shrine to put a yes-or-no question to a god. One convex and one flat is a yes; both flat is a no; some throws are void. It is the standard way of consulting a Chinese deity, and people still do it.

And the instrument is dangerous, because in this universe **the document is the act**. In 《城隍神酗酒》 a clerk writes a colleague's name upside down in red ink as a joke and burns the paper. That night the City God's bailiffs arrive. *Whenever a document arrives from the world of the living naming a man to be arrested, the underworld assists in arresting him.* The document existed; the naming existed; the man is guilty. The prank was written and then burned, and it made no difference. When he protests, the bailiffs offer him no justice — only a different desk: *汝只好到别衙门申冤*, you had better take your complaint to another yamen.

## A god can be wrong on purpose

The most quietly radical entry in this group is 《关神断狱》. A stolen chicken is judged by casting the moon-blocks at the shrine of Lord Guan. The blocks come up guilty — for the **wrong person**.

Years later the god explains himself, and the explanation is a magistrate's argument, not a priest's:

*我宁受不灵之名，以救生人之命 — I would rather be called ineffective than take a life.*

Heaven, we are told, praises him for it. Yuan Mei has put moral judgement above miraculous accuracy, which is rare in any tradition: the god is not honoured for being right. He is honoured for weighing the consequences of being believed.

## Suing for breach of contract

There is also a whole class of case in which nobody disputes that the god exists. They sue over **delivery**.

In 《牛头大王》 a god demands a temple in a dream and asks for 血食, meat offering, in exchange for a response. He delivers: the village epidemic clears, prayers for sons work, the cult runs for years. Then one father's boy dies after an auspicious divination, and the man takes a **hoe** to the statue.

What is striking is what he does *not* do. He does not deny the god for a moment. He disputes the performance. The complaint is breach of contract, not blasphemy — and the story treats smashing the idol as the correct filing of the appeal.

## The dead litigate, and they are good at it

Ghosts in these stories do not wail. They **plead**.

In 《盗鬼供状》 a revenue clerk is seized by the ankle by something rising out of the floor tiles: a robber he condemned in a former life, who has been screaming on the branding irons for sixty years. The ghost produces a **供状** — the formal written statement a criminal makes in a Qing court — and its argument is legal rather than emotional. It deserved death. But so did the man who took seven thousand ounces and promised to save it.

The complaint is not about the sentence. It is about the bribe. And the line that carries it is the most legally precise thing any ghost says in the whole collection:

*某不敢仇法吏，敢仇赃吏 — I do not dare to resent an officer of the law. I dare to resent a corrupt officer.*

Note the outcome: the judge who **wins** the argument loses the case. In this universe being right and having a remedy are two different things, which is a very Chinese thing to say about litigation.

Gods litigate too. In 《董贤为神》 an official decides a shrine to a beautiful young man in Han court dress must be bogus, and plans to pull the temple down for its timber. That night the god comes and defends his **reputation** — with textual criticism. The *Hanshu* says Emperor Ai was paralysed and could not beget children, and also that he favoured Dong Xian; the text contradicts itself, so the charge is unsound. He then cites an actual star, 幸臣, recorded near the Supreme Palace Enclosure: the category is cosmic, so the office is legitimate. A deity arguing from the star catalogue is the single most Chinese sentence in this essay.

## The profession this creates

If the system demands written plaints and almost nobody can write one, the system manufactures the man who writes it.

In 《讼师说讼》 a father and son who write lawsuits for hire are dragged before Yama, who asks how they do it. They explain at length, with examples — Liu Xiahui, who held a freezing woman in his lap all night without impropriety, argued as rape; Guan Zhong, granted a fief by his lord, charged under the statute on seizing farmland; and the reverse operation easier still.

The father's defence is comically honest: *do I love litigation? They come at me with gold and silk, and their eyes blaze, so I have to agree.*

Yama has the father's eyes gouged out and the son's arms cut off. They go back to work anyway — dictating and writing with their toes. The point lands harder than any moral: because the cosmos runs on documents, punishing the document-writer changes nothing.

## When the lawsuit is the revenge

Two stories carry the idea to its conclusion, and both refuse catharsis.

In 《奇女雪怨》 a gifted girl is betrayed by the scholar she has been tutoring. As a ghost she goes on tutoring him — all the way to a prefect's seat — specifically so that **the law, not she**, can execute him. Revenge in this universe is not a knife. It is a correctly routed case.

And in 《柳如是为厉》, the haunting raises a legal question rather than resolving one. Someone objects that Liu Rushi died a proper death for her minister, and *proper deaths do not produce hauntings*. Yuan Mei does not dismiss the objection; he answers it with a passage from the *History of Jin*. The ghost is arguing her standing to sue.

## Why it matters

Put the whole sequence together and you have the most distinctive thing about this universe, and the thing most likely to be missed by a reader arriving from Western fantasy: **the supernatural is not worshipped into submission, and it is not fought. It is served with process.**

This is what makes the cosmos in these stories feel, to a modern reader, less like a religion and more like a jurisdiction. There is an office for everything, an appeal route above every office, a written form for every grievance, and — as we argue in [the ledger](/worldview/the-ledger) — a statute that can be quoted back at the powers that issued it. When Heaven executes a sorcerer in 《李通判》, it writes the verdict on his corpse in characters of brimstone and closes it 如律令, *according to statute*.

Which leaves the last word where Pu Songling put it. At the close of 《席方平》 he remarks that if the world above worked like Er Lang's court, the world below would have no work at all — and then says, in his own voice, what he thinks of the courts of the living: that a poor man's lawsuit is a fire, and the clerks of this world are the ones who carry the oil.
