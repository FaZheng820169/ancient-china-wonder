In this universe the difference between the living and the dead is not only that one breathes and the other does not. It is also that one eats and the other cannot — or eats the wrong thing, or gets eaten. Across two thousand years of these tales, food is one of the main instruments by which the cosmos sorts its populations. Who may eat, what they eat, and whether eating is even possible where they are tells you which realm you are standing in.

## 1. The mouth that cannot close

The Buddhist cosmos the Chinese absorbed sorts beings into six paths. One of them is the **realm of hungry ghosts** (饿鬼道). Its inmates are not punished by fire so much as by appetite: mouths like needles, throats like hairs, bellies like hills, so that whatever they reach for turns to ash or is snatched away. Hunger is not a feeling they have; it is the condition they *are*.

The tradition keeps the category even while telling a thoroughly Chinese story. In **The Raksasa Bird**, Yuan Mei names the creature's class outright — yaksha, asura and **preta** (the hungry ghost) — and files the bird among them: a thing bred from the great yin of graveyards and the accumulated qi of piled-up corpses, *fond of eating human eyes*. The hungry ghost is not a foreign import parked at the edge of the map. It is the name the culture gives to appetite that has outlived its body.

## 2. Feeding the dead through fire

If the dead cannot eat on their own, the living feed them — but only across the one bridge that is safe: **fire**. You do not hand a ghost a meal. You burn it. Combustion is the border crossing; what goes up as paper and smoke comes down, in the other world, as the thing itself.

The logic runs both ways, and **The Ghost Who Borrowed a Title** is its comedy. A boy makes gold-foil hairpins and writes a false official document for a woman who says she is buying her daughter's dowry. She repays him with cakes and cash — which, by daylight, are lumps of earth and paper money. A child's playthings in an upper room became a ghost's dowry; the paper things are real enough in the underworld to be paid with, real enough here only to be exposed. The same fire that feeds the dead also unmasks them.

## 3. The meal you should not eat

The reverse crossing is the trap. To eat the food of the other world is to accept its claim on you. In **The Peach-Blossom Spring of Mount Tiantai**, two men lose their way and are fed by women who seem to have expected them: hemp-seed rice, goat's meat, hyssop cakes, wine. They stay what feels like half a year, ask to go home, and are let go — only to find seven generations have passed. The rule across the tradition is exact: eating the food of the immortals binds you to their time, or simply lets the ordinary world slide past without you. The meal is a contract you did not know you had signed.

And the dark mirror: sometimes it is the dead who eat *you*. In **The Zombie Who Drank the Blood**, a corpse with white hair all over its body holds a severed head and will not let go; when its arms are chopped, fresh blood pours out and the victim's head is dry, because the zombie sucked it dry. In **The Raksasa Bird**, the creature that stepped out of a grave takes a groom's eyes — the eyes being the one organ that tells true from false, and the one thing the eater cannot survive being looked at with. The boundary of the meal is the boundary of the self.

## 4. The ledger kept in calories

Because food is a unit of value as much as a substance, the afterlife keeps its accounts in it. A man's surplus profit is collected by a licensed ghost; a debt is settled not in coin but in edibles — seven taels of silver become a donkey, three hundred cash become thirty duck eggs. And the comedy has an earthly twin: in **The Man With No Vapour**, a schoolmaster so poor he is "outside the five elements" goes three months without the taste of meat, and only at the spring and autumn sacrifice to the duke does he get a properly cooked meal. The same scarcity that defines the hungry ghost defines, in miniature, the poor man who cannot afford to eat.

## Coda

What unites these is simple. In a universe where the body is made of qi and qi is the currency of existence, to be fed is to exist, and to be unfed is to be unfinished. The hungry ghost is appetite with no mouth to satisfy it. The sacrifice is food sent by fire. The forbidden meal is a claim you cannot undo. And the poor man, on the living side, is the hungry ghost's quiet twin. The cosmos does not separate its creatures by whether they are alive or dead. It separates them by whether they have been fed.
