Possession is one of the oldest plots in the world, and most traditions tell it the same way: a hostile spirit takes a body, the body's rightful owner is a victim, and a specialist arrives to drive the spirit out. The story is a moral contest, and it ends with an exorcism.

Read a few dozen Chinese tales on the same subject and you find something structurally different. The spirit is often not hostile, the original owner is often not there, and the ending is not an exorcism but a **settlement**. The question these stories ask is not *is this thing evil* but *who has the right to be in there?*

That is a question of property and contract, not of good and evil. Once you see it, a whole family of plots snaps into focus.

## The disputed corpse

《靈璧女借屍還魂》 is the hard case, and Yuan Mei reports it with the date and nothing else: no comment, no consolation.

A village woman dies. Some time later, in another family, a woman wakes up and announces:

> 吾某村中王姑娘也，尚未婚嫁，何為至此？吾之父母姊妹，俱在何處？
>
> I am Miss Wang of such-and-such a village. I am not married. How have I come here? Where are my parents and my sisters?

Her husband is horrified and sends word to the village, where the family is still weeping over their young daughter — whose body is already in the ground.

Two families then go to court over the same woman. The magistrate rules for the husband.

Note what the judge is actually deciding. He is not determining whether the woman is possessed, or whether the dead girl's claim is true. He is deciding **custody**. The body woke up in one household and is awarded to it. The tale ends there, and Yuan Mei files it as a 乾隆二十一年 matter of record.

## The fixed-term lease

Most borrowings in these stories are not seizures at all. They are agreements, and the terms are explicit.

In 《蘸婦冰心》 a woman forced into a second marriage dies of being called unchaste. Her friend is dying at the same hour, and the dead woman asks permission:

> 今姊生魂已游墟莽，妹欲借附尊躯，代守三十年苦節。
>
> Your living soul is already wandering the wastes. I wish to borrow and attach to your honoured body, and keep thirty years of bitter widowhood in your place.

Thirty years. Named. The friend consents, dies smiling, and the body gets up and announces it is going to keep its vow at the dead husband's house. Thirty years later, the task complete, the borrower explains herself to the brother, sits upright and dies.

《鬼婦持家》 is the same structure with a court order behind it. A dead father-in-law sentences his son's second wife to twelve years of instruction in the underworld, and lends her body to the dead first wife for the same twelve years:

> 即令新婦隨兒去，借渠手足，料理家務。俟兒女婚嫁畢，再當來此。
>
> Let the new wife go back with our son and borrow her hands and feet to manage the house. When the children are married off, she shall come back here.

Both sentences are served. On the day the children are settled, the first wife leaves the body; the second wife — now trained — comes back into it and runs the house properly for the rest of her life.

Two women, one body, two consecutive twelve-year terms, and a documented reason for each.

## The soul that simply leaves

Not every case involves taking someone else's body. 《離魂記》 is the clean version: Qianniang's soul leaves her body and follows the man she loves, while her body stays at home ill in bed. Years later the soul-body returns and the two rejoin.

No one is displaced. Nothing is contested. The point is the one that makes the whole category work: **a person and their body are separable, and the soul can be somewhere else while the body is still alive and still a person.**

## The evasion, and the counter

《藏魂壇》 is the only case in this group where the arrangement is criminal.

An unnaturally wicked son has, while still living, extracted his own soul and refined it into a jar:

> 逆子自知罪大惡極，故居家先將魂提出，煉藏壇內。官府所刑殺者，其血肉之體，非其魂也。
>
> The wicked son knew his crimes were extreme, so while still at home he drew his soul out first and refined it into a jar. What the authorities execute is his flesh and blood — not his soul.

The body can be beaten to death and heal in three days, because the refined soul repairs it. The counter is physical and immediate: find the jar, smash it. The proof that it worked is given in one clause — 不浹旬已臭腐, *it rotted within ten days*. Yuan Mei's evidence standard is the corpse.

So the soul can be **moved out of the body as insurance**, and the body can be kept running as a shell. There is a procedure for this, and there is a procedure against it.

## The queue at the sunbeam

Finally, the mechanics of getting a body at all. In 《鬼攀日線才能托生》 the dead of Yinshan wait for the winter solstice, when a single thread of sunlight reaches them:

> 群鬼蠕蠕然，僵而復動，攀日線而行，得至中國，復投人身。投做一人之身，常合群鬼而來，非止一人之魂也。
>
> The ghosts stir, stiff and then moving again, and climb the thread of sunlight until they reach the Middle Kingdom and are reborn into human bodies. Into one human body they often come as a crowd — it is not only one soul.

Two things here that no Western possession story contains. Rebirth is **physically difficult and has a schedule** — miss the thread and you wait another year. And a single human being routinely houses several souls, which is presented as ordinary, not as a crisis.

## Why the ending is a contract, not an exorcism

Line the cases up and the pattern is unambiguous:

| Case | Who occupies the body | Terms | How it ends |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 靈璧女 | A dead girl, in a buried body | Contested | Magistrate awards custody |
| 蘸婦冰心 | Borrower, with consent | Thirty years, named task | Term expires, borrower leaves |
| 鬼婦持家 | Borrower, by court order | Twelve years, then returned | Both terms served |
| 離魂記 | The owner's own soul, elsewhere | None | Rejoins |
| 藏魂壇 | Owner's soul removed to a jar | Evasion | Jar smashed |
| 攀日線 | Several souls at once | Rebirth queue | Ordinary |

The recurring elements are **consent, term, task, and consideration**. The recurring endings are expiry, settlement, or a counter-procedure. Almost never exorcism.

That difference matters if you are writing or running a game in this universe. A Chinese possession story is not a fight for a soul. It is a **dispute over occupancy** — and the interesting question is never how powerful the spirit is. It is whether the arrangement was agreed, what was promised, and what happens on the day the term runs out.
