The first question anyone asks about a ghost story is whether it is true. Chinese writers of the strange asked it themselves, at length, for about fourteen hundred years — and never once arrived at a common answer. What they produced instead is more interesting: four distinct, fully worked-out positions on what kind of knowledge a story about a ghost actually is.

You can tell them apart within a paragraph, and once you can, the whole tradition reorganises itself.

## 1. Gan Bao: I am proving it (4th century)

The *Soushen Ji* — *Records of Searching for the Spirits*, compiled around 350 — is where the genre starts, and its compiler states his purpose in the preface in five characters: 發明神道之不誣, to make clear that the way of spirits is not a lie.

That is an apologetic project, and it tells you what Gan Bao thought he was up against. He is not entertaining; he is producing evidence. The collection reads like a brief: case after case, short, plain, datable, attached to a place and a person, with the moral mechanism left visible. [Dong Yong](/stories/dong-yong) gets a weaving-woman because he is filial. [The Filial Wife of Donghai](/stories/donghai-xiaofu) is executed on a false accusation and the province goes dry for three years. Cause, effect, verification.

There is no narrator in these stories. That is the point. If the compiler's personality showed, the exhibit would be contaminated.

## 2. Hong Mai: the vice-prefect of Changzhou told me (12th century)

Eleven centuries later, Hong Mai spent sixty years assembling the *Yijian Zhi* — originally four hundred and twenty chapters, some two thousand seven hundred items, of which roughly half survives — and he did something nobody had done at scale.

**He named his informants.**

景裴弟倅貳建康，亦識之 — my younger brother Jing Pei, vice-administrator of Jiankang, knew the man. 其婿翁潾為饒州推官說此 — his son-in-law Weng Lin, a judicial officer at Raozhou, told me this. 余仲庸聞此事於敬夫 — Yu Zhongyong heard it from Zhang Shi himself. 紫槀長老清可親見之 — the elder Qingke of Zigao saw it with his own eyes.

This is reportage, and it changes everything about how the stories behave.

**He checks.** In [The Peacock Sutra](/stories/peacock-sutra) a plague demon, on its way out, says it is going to the silversmith's house next, and Hong Mai's man 走介扣曾匠家 — sends a runner to knock at the silversmith's door — 果云忽害傷寒 — and they say that he has indeed suddenly taken a chill. The story ends with a verification, not a moral.

**He prints rival accounts.** In [The Clerk Who Died Twice](/stories/the-clerk-who-died-twice) the haunting is elegantly resolved — the ghosts were an anxiety, the clerk was never dead, the magistrate recovers on the spot — and then Hong Mai adds: 或又言 — *but other people say* — that the clerk really did die and still comes out; that he saw him walk into the yamen years later; that the prefect had his servants beaten for not challenging a ghost; that the people of the district knew the old story and none of them dared tell him; 至今未能安泰云 — and to this day he has had no peace. Two endings, both printed, no adjudication. That is not a writer hedging. That is a writer keeping his sources distinct.

**He says when the verdict went too far.** In [The Ghost-Seer](/stories/the-ghost-seer) a dead maidservant avenges herself on her old mistress and on the man who merely passed on a recommendation. Hong Mai breaks in — which he almost never does — and calls it: 則為太濫矣. *That was excessive.* And then: 豈命數相值，偶然若是乎? Or was it simply that their allotted numbers fell out together? The collector refuses to defend his own material.

**He withholds judgement.** In [The Woman With the Cat](/stories/the-woman-with-the-cat) a woman is dragged before the prefect by a crowd of thousands, accused of being a tiger-spirit. She takes the brush and writes her own statement: my father was a magistrate, my husband is dead, I have no children, the young men of the market torment me and now they have decided I am an animal. The prefect lets her go. Later she is beaten for eating a dog. 後不知所在 — after that, nobody knew where she was. Hong Mai never tells you whether she was a tiger. He gives you the charge and the defence in the same room and leaves it there.

**And once, he puts himself in.** [The Candle-Flowers](/stories/the-candle-flowers) is the only entry in the collection where the compiler is a character in his own book: 紹興十五年三月十五日，予在臨安試詞科 — on the fifteenth day of the third month, Shaoxing 15, I sat the decree examination at Lin'an. Five candidates in a courtesan's upstairs room, two candles bloomed into flower, two song-lyrics written on the spot, one of them his. He got the degree; the other four did not. He reports it without triumph and without apology, and he kept the sheet.

The result of all this is a snapshot of how ordinary twelfth-century Chinese people actually imagined the supernatural — not what the priests taught, not what the philosophers conceded, but what a provincial clerk would repeat at dinner. Because the Song state ran on registers and warrants, the Song underworld does too. Read [The Warrant That Sank the Boat](/stories/the-warrant-that-sank-the-boat) and you have the whole thing in one scene: a rite performed correctly, a culprit produced, and a defence that consists entirely of a document from a higher office which the exorcist is obliged to accept.

## 3. Pu Songling: whatever you like (17th century)

Then comes the break, and it is a large one.

Pu Songling failed the examinations for the whole of his life and wrote some four hundred and ninety near-perfect classical stories about foxes, ghosts, examiners and flowers. He did not claim they happened. He signed them.

At the end of a great many *Liaozhai* pieces there is a line introduced by 異史氏曰 — the Historian of the Strange says — which is Pu borrowing, deliberately, the formula that the official historians used for their verdicts. He is putting his own commentary into the voice of a dynastic history for a collection he knows is fiction.

The shift is from record to invention, and you can watch it happen earlier, in the Tang. [The Governor of South Bough](/stories/nan-ke) and [The World Inside a Pillow](/stories/zhen-zhong) are not reports; they are signed constructions, and their authors put real, datable men into them for the pleasure of it. But the Tang writers still pretend. Pu stops pretending, and instead gives you something better: characters. Read [Nie Xiaoqian](/stories/nie-xiaoqian) and you have a ghost with a problem, a hero who is mostly useless, and a horror and a domesticity in the same room. That is not testimony about the spirit world. It is a novel in two thousand characters.

## 4. Yuan Mei: I don't believe it either (18th century)

The last position is the most modern-sounding, and it is the one that trips people up.

Yuan Mei was the greatest wit of the eighteenth century, a deliberate heretic, and the title of his collection — *Zibuyu*, 子不語 — quotes the *Analects*: the things the Master did not discuss. His stories are the funniest in the tradition and the most sceptical, and the two things are the same thing. He reports a wonder and takes the wonder-business apart in the same breath.

In [The Magistrate Who Went Down the Well](/stories/fengdu-magistrate) the presiding judge of the underworld denounces the priests who use gods to ruin families — which is a ghost story whose climax is an argument against the ghost trade. In [Governing Demons Is Easier](/stories/demons-are-easier) the point is made in the title. In [The Monster Feared Reason](/stories/monster-feared-reason) the monster is defeated by being argued at.

And Hong Mai, five centuries earlier, was already doing a version of this: the man who refuses the two tiger cubs in [The Woman With the Cat](/stories/the-woman-with-the-cat) says 吾豈應養虎自遺患 — why should I rear tigers to trouble myself with afterwards? Scepticism in these books is not a modern import. It is a native habit, and it is the reason the genre survived into a rationalist age.

## Why any of this matters to a reader now

Because "is it true" is the wrong question, and the writers knew it.

The four positions correspond to four kinds of knowledge, and all four are genuine:

- **Testimony** — a thing happened to a person, who told a person, who wrote it down with the chain of custody intact.
- **Argument** — a thing is claimed in order to establish a rule about how the world works.
- **Invention** — a thing is built to show you what people are like.
- **Refusal** — a thing is reported and then declined, so that you can hear both the claim and the objection.

Once you sort a collection by which of these it is doing, the tradition stops being a heap of marvels and becomes something you can actually read. [Tan Sheng](/stories/tan-sheng) is testimony with a rule attached — do not look, and here is what happens if you do. [The Death-God in the Cangue](/stories/death-god-cangue) is an argument about office. [The Fox Who Debated the Classics](/stories/fox-debated-classics) is a comedy with a thesis. [The Ghost-Seer](/stories/the-ghost-seer) is testimony that the collector himself declines to endorse.

## What this site does with it

Every story here is translated **from the Chinese original only** — never from an existing English version, and never from a retelling. That rule exists because of the argument above. If a genre's whole interest is *what kind of claim it is making*, then the source language is where the claim lives: in the sentence where Hong Mai names his informant, in the aside where he calls a verdict excessive, in the second ending he prints because somebody else told it differently.

So where the original gave us a name, we keep the name. Where the original gave two endings, we give you both. Where the original refused to say whether the woman was a tiger, we don't say either.
