  文登，字道岸，浙之武康人。十七游庠，聘某氏，未嫁而夭，郁郁不自得。浪迹出游，将为求凰计。偶至凤阳，遇道者于涂，诘其所自，生告以意。道者曰：“汝欲得佳妇，此去东南十五里外，往求之，必有所遇。”生信之，如所指而行。至，则春台演剧，观者蜂屯蚁聚，无可停趾。回视垂杨低处，露小红楼一角，有女子搴帘，衣半折，侧面偷窥。粉光黛影，射人双目。生回旋顾盼，几难自主，迨斜日西倾，歌场乐阙，犹仰面空楼，初不觉游人尽散也。忽一人拍肩大喝曰：“何物痴儿，窥人闺阁？”生视之，岸然伟丈夫，竟拉其臂，强曳登堂。生两股战栗，变色欲走。因大笑曰：“如此胆怯，也学风狂。实相告，楼头女子，即仆掌珠。君如闺中无妇，愿附婚姻。”生变惧为喜，唯唯惟命。
  时已秉烛，令女子严装讫，与生交拜，拥入闲房，将攀情话。
  俄母氏招女去。生兀坐灯下，意绪无聊。漏下二鼓，见画屏东畔女子独来，对镜卸翠凤翘，金雀花双朵，旋解芙蓉帔。鸳鸯百折裙，斜倚床阑，脱藕覆，褪双丝文绣履，兜三寸许软红睡鞋，低鬟一笑，光入重帏。生欲焰中烧，不能自制，而登床急抱，阒其无人，唯绣枕横陈，半堆锦被而已。大骇，莫详其故。拥被孤眠，旁皇终夜。侵晓，女子即来。生诘之，默然不答。
  至夜，生先匿锦帐中。更阑后，女子衣短红袄，外系金鸾紫络带，发惺松作懒装，兜以皂帕；下体绣裙不掩，露绛直文罗裤，提缕金鞋刬袜而来，披帏竟登床榻。生急捉其臂，随手转侧，如一团绛雪，飞堕巫山；索之，悄然无迹矣！是仙是鬼，益莫测其踪影。三竿日出，候女不至。
  女之妹颖姑，偶过其室。生正苦岑寂，于镜旁舐毫作字。颖姑睇而门曰：“尔亦曾读书识字耶？”生曰：“予虽不肖，束发游庠岂有秀才家不读书识字者？”颖姑失声一叹。生疑之，再三絮问，颖姑曰：“吾怜汝青年秀士，死期已逼，尚不自知。”生长跪请教，曰：“吾家翁姥，专以左道劫人财物。将欲举事，必先杀一人，祀神开路。往往悬姊为饵，名曰夫妇，而实一无所染。吾自有知识以来，见其出衽席而登俎上者，不知几千百儿郎矣！今夜明星烂时，殆将及汝。”
  生窘极，叩首乞援。颖姑曰：“吾何能救汝？欲解倒悬，还须阿姊。”生问计。曰：“姊所以登床即遁者，固褥底压六甲符一通，上缠灯绿丝三十六缕。汝搜括而弃之，彼必不能脱身。苟得成其夫妇，而后以情义哀之，自能免汝于难。”生谨受教。颖姑潜引去。生启视褥底，果如所言，急弃之。
  入夜女来，伺其缓装登榻，裸而就之。女意似觉，曰：“婢子多言，败我家事。虽然，亦天意也。”纵体投怀，竟成欢会。事讫，裸跪床头，哀其援手，女曰：“百年伉俪，万死相随，何待君言？”
  急起，以雄鸡系于杖头，嘱生肩荷之，曰：“往北约行三十里，俟鸡声一唱，即舍之而走，再行二十余里，待妾来时，好共发也。”生谨记而去。
  女佯告诸父。父大怒，跨马欲追。女曰：“追之不获，不如飞剑以斩。”父从女言，掷剑于庭，去同白练。亡何，电光一闪，铮然堕地，血涔涔斑痕犹湿也。
  时生出北郭门，约行三十里，杖头鸡声大作，急委之于地。瞥白光下注，而鸡寂热无声矣。又行二十余里，筋力已疲，憩于树下，见云中一鹤飞堕，女已控背而来。敛之，一纸鹤耳！笑曰：“大劫已过，请归乡土。”生曰：“奈汝父何？”女曰：“左道无长策。五十里外，不能及也。”候晓而行，不匝月，偕归故云。生键户读书，暇辄与女藏阄为乐。
  一日，有女子闯然而入，视之，颖姑也。并起问故。颖姑曰：“自姊去后，父母强妹为代。妹意不屑为，至逢其怒，日遭鞭挞。幸老父赴天魔会去，乘间而逃。复思伶仃弱质，绝无亲串可依，故一路间程，相投至此。”女大喜。生曰：“姨来亦大好。但非鸦非凤，卿家何以位置？”女笑曰：“我本无猜，君宜报德。台上英、皇，其例自可援也。”即出簪珥，为颖姑上头。颖姑赪颜却之，曰：“妹子此来，不过作闲门冷燕，岂求野鸭入鸳鸯队乎？”女以正言谕之，始无异议。
  正曳令交拜，有道者自外而来，笑曰：“得妇之言，今颇验否？”生敬谢之。
  二女相顾，骇曰：“似吾父之师也。”道者曰：“然！尔父学仙不成，流为左道，而复借吾教中飞符遁甲诸术，日济其恶。痛加训诲，罔有悛心，必至一朝翦灭。因惜女子无辜，亦遭惨戮，故引文郎入幕，转辗相援，脱汝等于水火中耳！”女问：“父母无恙否？”道者曰：“此刻一番闲话，即汝全家就缚时也。”二女大哭，道者曰：“是渠恶报，何哭为？”拂袖竟去。
  后生密探其耗，果于是日为官军搜捕，骈首西郊，益信道者之神也。
  铎曰：“化人城里，不少魔关，然鬼母儿孙，终入大菩萨莲花钵底。一日回头，同依道岸。二女之得脱，是借仙家妙指，而离佛门苦劫者。行险侥幸，今古有几人哉？”

Wen Deng, courtesy name Dao'an, was a man of Wukang in Zhejiang. At seventeen he entered the district school; he was betrothed, and the girl died before the wedding, and he went about grey with it. He took to wandering, meaning to look for a wife elsewhere.

At Fengyang he met a Daoist on the road who asked where he was bound. Wen told him. The Daoist said: "If you want a good wife, go fifteen li to the south-east and ask there. You will meet someone."

Wen believed him and went as directed. He arrived in the middle of an opera at the spring stage, the crowd packed like bees and ants, nowhere to set a foot. Looking round, he saw below the drooping willows the corner of a little red tower, and a woman holding the curtain aside, half-turned, peeping sideways. Powder and dark brows struck his eyes. He circled and stared, hardly master of himself; and when the sun slanted west and the singing ended he was still gazing up at the empty tower, not noticing that the crowd had gone.

Suddenly a hand clapped his shoulder and a voice roared: "What sort of fool is this, peeping into a woman's rooms?"

Wen turned. It was a tall, imposing man, who took his arm and dragged him into the hall. Wen's legs shook; he changed colour and tried to run. The man laughed.

"So faint-hearted, and yet you play the mad gallant. I will tell you plainly. The girl in the tower is the pearl of my house. If you have no wife at home, I am willing to make a marriage of it."

Wen's fear turned to joy, and he agreed to everything.

By candlelight the girl was dressed in full, the two of them bowed to each other, and he was pushed into a side room. Then her mother called her away. Wen sat alone under the lamp. At the second watch the girl came by herself from the east of the painted screen, and at the mirror took down her kingfisher phoenix pin and her two gold sparrow flowers, and loosed the hibiscus cape and the hundred-fold mandarin-duck skirt, leaning against the bed-rail, and slipped on soft red sleeping-shoes of three inches, and smiled with her head bent, and the light went into the inner curtain.

Wen burned. He could not stop himself; he leapt onto the bed and caught at her — and there was no one. Only an embroidered pillow lay across it, and a heap of brocade quilt. He was terrified and could not begin to account for it, and lay awake all night hugging the quilt. At dawn the girl came. He questioned her. She was silent.

That night Wen hid first in the bed-curtains. Late, the girl came in a short red jacket with a gold-phoenix purple cord over it, her hair loose, a black kerchief, and she put aside the curtain and got onto the bed. Wen grabbed her arm — and she turned under his hand like a ball of crimson snow and was gone, silently, without a trace. Immortal or ghost, he could not guess which.

The girl's younger sister Yinggu happened to pass his room. Wen, in his misery, was wetting a brush by the mirror to write. Yinggu looked and said: "You can read and write, then?"

Wen said: "Poor as I am, I entered the school at fifteen. What scholar's house has a man who cannot read?"

Yinggu gave a cry and a sigh. He pressed her, and at last she said: "I pity you — a young man of talent, with your death already close, and you do not know it."

Wen knelt to be taught.

"My father and mother live by the left way, taking people's goods by force. Before every job they must kill one man, to sacrifice and open the road. They hang my sister out as bait — it is called marriage, but she is never actually touched. Ever since I could understand anything, I have watched young men go from that bed to the chopping-block — I do not know how many hundreds. Tonight, when the bright stars blaze, it will be your turn."

Wen was desperate and knocked his head for help.

"I cannot save you. To loosen the noose you need my sister herself."

She told him the trick: "The reason she vanishes the moment she is on the bed is that a liujia talisman is pressed under the mattress, wound about with thirty-six strands of lamp-green silk. Search it out and throw it away, and she will not be able to get free. If you become man and wife in truth, and then plead with her out of that bond, she will get you out of this."

Yinggu slipped away. Wen lifted the mattress. It was exactly as she had said. He threw the thing out.

That night the girl came. He waited until she had undressed and got onto the bed, and held her; and she seemed to know.

"That chattering child has ruined my family's business. And yet — it is heaven's will too."

Afterwards Wen knelt naked at the bed-head and begged her to save him. She said: "A hundred years of marriage, and I follow you through ten thousand deaths. Why did you need to ask?"

She rose, tied a rooster to the end of a staff, and told him to carry it on his shoulder. "Go north about thirty li. The moment the rooster crows, drop it and run on. Twenty li further, wait for me, and we will go on together."

She then told her father, pretending alarm. Her father flew into a rage and mounted to pursue. She said: "You will not catch him. Better to cut him down with the flying sword." He took her advice and flung the sword into the courtyard; it went out like a white ribbon.

Wen had left the north gate and gone some thirty li when the rooster on the staff crowed loudly. He threw it down. A white light stabbed downward, and the rooster was silent. Another twenty li and his strength failed; he rested under a tree, and saw a crane fall out of the cloud with the girl on its back. Folded up, it was a paper crane.

She laughed: "The great calamity is past. Let us go home."

Wen said: "But what of your father?"

She said: "The left way has no long reach. Beyond fifty li it cannot touch us."

Within the month they were back in his own country. He locked his door and read; in his leisure he played guessing-games with his wife.

One day a girl burst in. It was Yinggu. They asked why. She said: "After my sister left, my parents forced me to take her place. I would not, and it angered them, and I was whipped daily. Luckily my father went off to a demon assembly, and I escaped in the interval. I am alone and weak, with no kin to turn to, so I came all this way to you."

The wife was overjoyed. Wen said: "Your sister coming is well. But neither crow nor phoenix — how shall we place her?"

His wife smiled: "I am not the jealous sort, and you owe her a debt. The precedent of Ehuang and Nüying is there to follow." She brought out hairpins and did Yinggu's hair for the wedding.

Yinggu blushed and refused: "I came to be a cold swallow at a quiet gate, not a wild duck joining the mandarin flock." It took plain arguing before she agreed.

They were just about to make them bow to each other when a Daoist came in from outside, smiling: "My word about finding a wife — has it proved out?"

The two girls looked at each other and said in alarm: "This is our father's master."

He said: "It is. Your father failed at studying immortality and slid into the left way, and then borrowed the flying-talisman and遁甲 arts of my teaching to serve his wickedness every day. I corrected him sharply; he has not repented in the least, and one day he must be wiped out. I grudged that the girls, who are innocent, should be slaughtered too. So I drew this young gentleman into the net, and by roundabout means pulled you both out of fire and water."

The girl asked: "Are my parents unharmed?"

The Daoist said: "In this very moment of idle talk, your whole family is being bound." The two girls wept. He said: "It is their own evil coming home. Why weep for them?" And he shook his sleeve and left.

Wen afterwards quietly enquired, and indeed on that day the family was hunted down by government troops and beheaded at the western suburbs.

*The Duo comment: In the city of the transformed there are many demon-gates; but the ghost-mother's children end in the great bodhisattva's lotus bowl. One day they turn their heads, and both rest on the shore of the Way. That the two girls got free was by a immortal's subtle finger, lifting them out of a bitter kalpa. To run such risk and win — how many, in any age, have managed it?*
