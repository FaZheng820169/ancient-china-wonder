  - term: "The posting announced before death"
    text: "我是燕子磯水神，今將復位 — I am the water god of Swallow Rock, and I am now to take up my post again. The verb is 復位, resume a post, and it makes the whole matter an appointment rather than a transformation. He is not becoming a god; he is going back to a station he held before, and this life was the interval. Compare 《董賢為神》 and 《護國大將軍》 for other men who turn out to have been on secondment, and note how ordinary the tone is — the minister delivers this the way a man tells his family where the deeds to the house are kept."
  - term: "A lot as proof of office"
    text: "死後，汝等送靈柩江西，必過此磯，有關帝廟，可往求籤。如係上上第三籤者，我仍為水神。否則，或有譴謫，不能復位矣 — after I die, when you carry my coffin to Jiangxi you must pass this rock, and there is a temple of Lord Guan; go and draw a lot. If it is the third lot of the highest grade, then I am water god still. If not, then perhaps I have been censured and reduced, and cannot resume the post. This is the most bureaucratic deathbed instruction in the collection: a dying man arranging his own verification. He does not ask to be believed. He specifies a test, names the grade, names the number, and leaves open the possibility of demotion — 有譴謫, a term from the personnel code. Posthumous divinity here is an office with an examination result, and his family are handed the means of checking it."

The dream-god's promise of two sons, one of them good, and the birth that follows, turn the god's office into a family arrangement: the temple asked for a boy, got one, and the man is now returning the post he was lent."
  - term: "A tablet, not an idol"
    text: "遂舉家大哭，燒紙錢蔽江，立木主於廟旁 — the whole household wept aloud, burnt paper money until it covered the river, and set up a wooden spirit tablet beside the temple. What they install is a 木主, the plain ancestral tablet of a dead man, not a statue of a god; and they install it 於廟旁, beside the shrine, not in it. The distinction is worth holding on to. The family is not making an idol of him, they are placing his ancestral seat next to a deity's house, which is how a private dead man is eased into a public cult — and it is also how a shrine acquires a second, newer occupant without anyone having to demote the first. Compare 《順濟王》 and 《毀陳友諒廟》."
  - term: "The epigraphy accumulates"
    text: "旁有尹文端公詩碣 — and beside it is a slab bearing a poem by Lord Yin Wenduan. The shrine is only years old and it already has a stone monument from a grand secretary, and by the end of the tale it has Yuan Mei's poem on the temple wall as well. This is exactly how these sites actually grew: a tablet, then a poem, then a slab, then a reputation, and a later traveller treats the growing pile of inscriptions as the proof that the god is real. The tale is quietly showing us the mechanism by which a dead official becomes a local institution, and Yuan Mei is not observing it from outside — he is one of the contributors."
  - term: "The poem and the wind"
    text: "予往蘇州，阻風於此，乃揖其主而題壁曰…次日果大順風 — going to Suzhou, I was held up by the wind here; so I bowed to his tablet and wrote on the wall… and the next day there was a great fair wind indeed. The four closing lines of the poem are the point: 江神如識我，應送好風多, if the river god has any knowledge of me, he ought to send me a good deal of fair wind. A scholar pays a courtesy to a god he has just been writing about, states his request in the conditional, and is answered. Whether the wind is a miracle or a coincidence is left standing, and Yuan Mei — who has already told us he half believed nothing and half believed everything — signs his name to neither."

Lord Qiu Wenda, when he was about to die, said to his family:

"I am the water god of Swallow Rock, and I am now to take up my post again. After I die, when you carry my coffin to Jiangxi you are bound to pass this rock. There is a temple of Lord Guan there; go and draw a lot. If it is the third lot of the highest grade, then I am water god still. If it is not, perhaps I have been censured and reduced, and cannot resume the post."

Having said this, he died.

His family heard it and half believed it, but one old servant believed him firmly and said: "My lord was born to the Lady Wang. The lady was of Jiangning by origin, and when she crossed the river she prayed for a son at the water god's temple at Swallow Rock. That night she dreamed that a man in robe and tablet came to her and said: I give you a son, and a good son along with him. And in a little over a year my lord was born."

Lord Qiu's wife, the Lady Xiong, carried the coffin home. When they reached Swallow Rock they did as he had told them and drew a lot in the temple of Lord Guan, and there was indeed the third lot. The whole household wept aloud, and burnt paper money until it covered the river, and set up a wooden spirit tablet beside the temple. Beside it stands a slab bearing a poem by Lord Yin Wenduan.

When I was going to Suzhou I was held up by the wind at this very place, so I bowed to his tablet and wrote on the wall:

*Moored along the bank at Swallow Rock,*
*I pass beneath the Huanggong wine-shop.*
*I rub my hand over the old inscription*
*and grieve for these hills and rivers.*
*My short hair is grizzled white as snow;*
*the long river runs off into faint waves.*
*If the river god has any knowledge of me,*
*he ought to send me a good deal of fair wind.*

The next day there was a great fair wind indeed.
