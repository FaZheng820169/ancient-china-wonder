  秦川女子霍小媖，有殊色。父与豪右某争田界，以他事诬诸官，竟毙于狱。母痛哭曰：“家无男子，谁为父复仇者？恐白骨冤埋，终作千秋黑狱矣！”女含涕而进曰：“儿不肖，髫龄稚齿，不能作赵家娥。有得仇人而杀之者，儿愿执箕帚事之。”母鉴其诚，日以其言祷诸西山之麓。
  一日，闻某入城祝县令寿，路出西山，虎突起于前，啮喉而毙。母女方额手庆，忽一虎曳尾而来，径登堂上。母女变色却走。虎徘徊瞻眺，殊无恶意。母阖扉而语曰：“今日杀某于道者，非汝也耶？”虎颔之。母曰：“蒙君仗义，雪我前仇。茕茕母女，定当香花顶礼，用酬大德。未识降临玉趾，意欲何为？”虎怒目而视，似憎其爽约者。母曰：“汝以我食言耶？息壤在彼，本宜敬将幼女侍奉裳衣。但起居寝食，彼此道殊。安得竟成伉俪？况我年近桑榆，家无兰玉，方将倚婿为活。汝为地下人报怨，独不为未亡人施德乎？谨陈衷曲，乞赐矜全。”
  虎闻其语，神凋气丧，垂头欲出；而一步九顾，依依不舍。女慷慨面前曰：“君且住。妾有一言，幸垂明听。妾前以身相许，岂敢昧心。想衾裯之共，君亦知其不可。如不忘旧约，当扫除一室，与君终身相守，存夫妇之名可也。”虎首肯再三，欣然嘉纳。
  女乃导虎入帷，营菟裘于绣榻之旁；食则同牢，居则同室。女晨起理妆，虎必潜身奁次，侧目偷窥。夜俟女卸装登床就寝，始伏于床下，竟夕不寐。恐以鼾声扰其清梦也。有时甘旨不给，则衔鹿脯以进，或抱小恙，焦思躁急，盘旋室内者无停趾。病愈，始欢跃如初。女习以为常。
  而母氏因年迈无依，时咎女之失计，而遇虎礼貌亦衰。虎一夕竟去。母欲为择婿。女曰：“背德不祥，负恩非福：况女子以心许人，岂必作形骸之论哉？”执不允。后女以郁疾死，停尸堂上。虎忽嗥哭而来，泪下如雨，进殓者皆见之。继埋玉于祖茔之侧，虎一日巡视者三。春秋令节，辄衔山果以奠。越三载如一日。母贫乏不能自话，虎犹日取山獐野兔，存恤其家云。

notes:
  - term: "Zhao's E (赵家娥)"
    text: "The girl says she is too young to be a Zhao E — Zhao E being the Eastern Han woman who, when her father was wrongly killed, slew his murderer herself and then surrendered to the authorities. Shen Qifeng uses her as the type of the filial female avenger, and the joke is that Huo Xiaoying's weapon is not a blade but a marriage vow."
  - term: "A pledge to a beast"
    text: "Promising a daughter to whoever avenges a father is an old motif (the 'reward for the head' tale), but here the avenger is an animal. The story keeps the question open — is the tiger a beast, a wronged spirit in beast form, or simply the instrument the cosmos sends? Shen never says, which is why it stays strange."
  - term: "Sharing a name, not a quilt"
    text: "The girl refuses the literal marriage ('sharing the quilt') but keeps the tiger as 'husband' in name, sleeping by her bed and bringing her meat. The arrangement is chastely domestic, and the mother's objection — 'our ways of living are different' — is the only place the story admits the gap between species."
  - term: "Foolish yet proud (痴而能傲)"
    text: "The closing 铎曰 turns on a paradox: the tiger loses its tiger-nature by being lovestruck, yet leaves in a huff at a cross word — which is exactly tiger-nature, untamable. 'Foolish and able to be proud is true pride; proud and able to be foolish is true foolishness.' The line is the story's real subject: devotion that will not be managed."

A girl of Qinchuan named Huo Xiaoying was of extraordinary beauty. Her father quarrelled with a local magnate over a field boundary; the magnate framed him on another charge, and the old man died in prison. The mother wept: "We have no man in the house — who will avenge my husband? I fear his white bones will lie unjustly buried, a black gaol for a thousand autumns!"

The daughter, with tears, stepped forward: "Your unworthy child is young and small, unable to be a Zhao E. But whoever kills the enemy, I will serve him with broom and tray."

Moved by her sincerity, the mother daily prayed this vow at the foot of the western mountain.

One day the magnate went to town to celebrate the magistrate's birthday and passed the western mountain. A tiger leapt out of the brush and bit his throat, killing him. Mother and daughter were rejoicing — when suddenly a tiger came trailing its tail and walked straight up into the hall. They paled and shrank back. But the tiger only wandered, looking about, with no malice at all.

The mother closed the door and said: "Was it not you who killed the man on the road today?"

The tiger nodded.

"You have done us a righteous service in avenging our wrong. We orphaned mother and daughter will surely burn incense and bow to repay your great virtue. But what brings you to our door — and what do you wish?"

The tiger glared, as if angered that she had broken her word.

"Do you think I broke my promise? The covenant stands — I should indeed present my daughter to tend your clothes. But our ways of sleeping and eating are different. How could we become man and wife? And I am near my declining years, with no son to lean on; I had meant to rely on a son-in-law for support. You avenged the dead — will you not also show mercy to the living?"

At this the tiger's spirit fell; it drooped its head, about to leave — yet at every step it looked back nine times, reluctant to part.

The girl stepped boldly forward. "Stay. I have a word. I pledged myself to you before; how dare I forget? Sharing a quilt — you know that cannot be. But if you do not forget the old vow, I will clear a room and keep you company all my life, keeping the name of husband and wife."

The tiger nodded again and again, and accepted gladly.

She led it behind the curtain and made a lair by the embroidered couch; they ate at the same table and lived in the same room. When she rose to dress, the tiger hid by the toilet-case, peeping sideways. At night it waited until she had undressed and gone to bed, then lay under the bed, awake the whole night, fearing its snore would disturb her pure dream. When choice food ran short it carried deer-meat to her; if she fell a little ill it paced the room in anguish, and only rejoiced when she recovered. She grew used to it.

But the mother, old and helpless, began to blame the girl for her poor choice, and her courtesy to the tiger waned. One night the tiger left. The mother wanted to find a real husband. The girl said: "To betray a virtue is ill-omened; to fail a kindness is no blessing. And a woman who pledges her heart to a man — must it come to a matter of bodies?" She would not consent.

Later the girl died of a broken heart and lay in the hall. The tiger came howling and weeping, tears like rain; all who dressed her coffin saw it. She was buried by the ancestral grave, and the tiger came to watch three times a day. On every festival it carried mountain fruit to lay on her mound. For three years, as one day. And when the mother sank into poverty and could not keep herself, the tiger still brought roe-deer and hares daily to sustain the household.

*The Duo comment: One who is love-foolish must lack a proud bone. A tiger that is foolish has lost its tiger-nature. Yet at one word's discord it turns its head and leaves — is that not still the tiger-nature, hard to tame? Foolish yet able to be proud is true pride; proud yet able to be foolish is true foolishness.*
