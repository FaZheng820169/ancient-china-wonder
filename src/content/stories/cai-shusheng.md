notes:
  - term: "Seeking a substitute"
    text: "The belief behind every Chinese hanging ghost: a person who dies by suicide cannot leave until someone else dies the same way in the same place. The ghost is not malice — it is a prisoner with one possible key, and it has to trick someone into turning it. Hence the beckoning, the second rope, the whole pantomime."
  - term: "Why 'I have made no mistake' wins"
    text: "Cai's answer is not a spell, a talisman, or a sword. It is a logical objection. The trap requires the victim to choose the noose freely; Cai walks into it and then declines the premise, and the mechanism has nothing left to grip. Compare the rule quoted in 李通判 in the same collection: 死人还阳，须得替代 — for a dead man to return to the light, someone must take his place."
  - term: "The red silk"
    text: "The trailing red silk (红帛) is the signature costume of the Chinese hanging ghost, and the colour is doing double duty: red is the wedding colour and the colour that repels demons, so the figure of the bride and the figure of the corpse are the same figure. In Chinese folklore the suicide ghost is conventionally young, female, and dressed for a wedding she will not have."
  - term: "Yuan Mei's compression"
    text: "Whole 聊斋 tales are built out of a scene this size. Yuan Mei gives us the haunted house, the ghost, the exchange, the outcome, and the afterwards in about a hundred words — and then one dry line: 蔡亦登第, Cai also took his degree. The reward for not being fooled is an ordinary career."

Outside the north gate of Hangzhou there was a house where a ghost appeared so often that no one would live in it, and the door had stood locked and barred for years.

A scholar named Cai decided to buy it. Everyone warned him. He did not listen.

The deed was signed. His family refused to set foot inside. Cai opened the house himself, lit a candle, and sat down.

At midnight a woman drifted in, slowly, with a length of red silk trailing from her neck. She knelt to Cai. Then she tied a rope to the beam and put her head into the noose.

Cai showed no fear.

She tied a second rope and beckoned to him.

Cai dragged one foot forward and put his head into it.

"You have made a mistake," said the woman.

Cai laughed.

"You were the one who made a mistake — that is why you are what you are today. I have made none."

The ghost wailed. She prostrated herself twice and was gone.

After that the haunting stopped. Cai took his degree. Some say he was Cai Binghou, the provincial treasurer.
