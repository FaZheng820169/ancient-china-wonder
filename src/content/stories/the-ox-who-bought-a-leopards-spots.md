  通谱之风，莫盛于江左。有某姓者，门户式微，以负贩起家；意欲攀援仕族，商诸比部吴君；吴善讽刺，曰：“我有一典，请为汝述之。”某肃然敬听，吴曰：“昔河鼓贳玉帝聘钱，谪居营室。后勤于耕获，积金钱数万，捆载牛背，赴天门先行缴纳，而牛忽奔逸下界，自顾形秽，不堪震俗。因念背上物颇充积，不难依附华族，夸耀乡里。往东海谒麒鳞，告以意。麟曰：‘予之角，振振公族；予之趾，振振公子。且一角五蹄，代生异相。岂汝触墙成字者，能溷乃公种类乎？’叱之去。又诣西域，投青狮座下，未及通谒。狮见其状貌蠢劣，大声一吼，遗粪满地，辟易数千里外，踯躅荒野，无所适从。

  忽忆庐山长耳公，当日有同车之谊，往筹之。长耳公曰：‘此间南山有金钱豹者，虽托名雾隐，而实广为结纳。仆请为介，必蒙收录。’遂同诣南山。长耳公先道达诚意。豹曰：‘物以类聚。与足下交者，大都彭亨腹涨者也。’长耳公极称其可，引牛进见登堂局蹐，终惭不类。豹初拒之，继见其所负金钱，笑曰：‘相君之背，富不可言。且我家所以称豹变者，因背有金钱文耳！若虽不由天贼，尚可借人力为之。’命出其金钱，引皮上毛，编辑成文。亡何，异色斑烂，金光闪烁，居然具体而微，不似管中窥者，仅见一斑也。长耳公熟视，笑曰：‘一破悭囊，便成俊物。虽介葛卢来，亦闻声莫辨矣！’遂别去。豹自此引为同类，而牛亦掉尾自雄，日随步后尘，焜耀长林丰草间。不匝旬，金钱尽脱，皮毛如旧。豹怒曰：‘如此丑态，玷我华宗。’喧逐之，牛彷徨无措，仍投斗篷宫来。河鼓以珊瑚鞭捶背者百。继诘其金钱何在。牛具告。河鼓曰：‘蠢哉畜类！若辈所愿与汝联宗者，缘汝数万金钱耳！一旦金钱罄尽，尚肯引泥涂中物为祖若父之贤子孙哉？’以铁索贯其鼻，系诸牢筴之中。后人遂名河鼓曰‘牵牛。’”

  某闻之汗流满额，而通谱之兴索矣！

  铎曰：“负薪实廉吏后人，皂隶亦贵卿末裔。乃以遥遥华胄，薄己祖宗，冒人孙子，吾不识其是何肺肠？然元宰升庸，诸狐带令，本非一姓，尚以攀附为荣，又何怪乎同姓而议宗者？”

The fashion of joining genealogies is nowhere more rampant than west of the river. There was one of a certain surname whose house had declined and who had risen by peddling, and who wanted to climb into the society of official families. He took counsel with Master Wu of the Bureau of Judgements.

Wu was a man who enjoyed mockery. He said: "I have an old tale. Let me tell it to you." The man listened with great respect.

Wu said: "Long ago the Ox-Herd borrowed the Jade Emperor's bride-price money and was banished to the Encampment. Afterwards he grew diligent at ploughing and harvesting, and piled up tens of thousands in cash. These he bound on an ox's back and carried to the Gate of Heaven to pay in before his time.

"But the ox suddenly broke away and fled down into the lower world. It looked at itself and felt that its form was too foul to face a crowd. Then, remembering that the load on its back was rather substantial, it thought it would not be hard to attach itself to a noble clan and show off in its village.

"It went to the Eastern Sea and sought out the qilin, and told it what it wanted. The qilin said: 'My horns are the noble clan; my toes are the noble sons — and one horn and five toes beget a marvellous form generation after generation. Can you, who make characters by butting your head against a wall, muddy my lord's kind?' And it shouted the ox away.

"It went on to the Western Regions and sought shelter under the throne of the Blue Lion. Before it could announce itself, the lion saw its stupid and clumsy looks, gave a great roar, dropped dung all over the ground, and drove it off thousands of li. There it wandered the wilderness with nowhere to turn.

"Then it recalled the Long-Ear Lord of Mount Lu, with whom it had once shared a cart, and went to plan with him. The Long-Ear Lord said: 'Here in the southern mountains there is one called the Golden-Coin Leopard, who claims to live in the mist but in fact takes in a wide acquaintance. Let me introduce you; you will surely be accepted.'

"So they went together to the southern mountains, and the Long-Ear Lord first conveyed the ox's wish. The leopard said: 'Things gather by kind. Those who mix with you are for the most part the bloated-bellied sort.' The Long-Ear Lord praised the ox to the skies, and brought him in. He came into the hall with awkward little steps, ashamed the whole time of being unlike.

"The leopard at first refused him. Then, seeing the money he carried on his back, it laughed: 'Looking at your back, your wealth is beyond words. And the reason my house is called the leopard's transformation is that our backs bear the golden-coin pattern. Yours was not made by Heaven's hand — but it can still be made by human effort.'

"It ordered the money brought out, and drawing up the hair of the hide, arranged it into a pattern. Before long the skin was dappled with strange colours and glittered with gold, and it was quite a leopard, only small — not the single spot that one sees through a tube.

"The Long-Ear Lord looked steadily at him, and laughed: 'One broken money-box, and you are a fine creature. Even Jie Gelu could not tell you apart by your voice now.'

"So it took its leave. From that time on the leopard treated the ox as one of its own kind, and the ox too wagged its tail and called itself heroic, following in the leopard's dust day after day and shining among the tall woods and the rich grass.

"But before ten days were out the golden coins had all come away and the hide was as it had been. The leopard raged: 'Such an ugly condition disgraces my noble line.' And it drove him out with a great noise.

"The ox wandered with nowhere to go, and went back to the Palace of the Dipper-Cloak. The Ox-Herd beat his back with a coral whip a hundred times, and then demanded where the money was. The ox told him everything.

"The Ox-Herd said: 'Stupid beast. The only reason those creatures wished to join clans with you was your tens of thousands of cash. Once the money is gone, will they still lead a thing out of the mud to be the worthy descendant of their fathers and grandfathers?'

"And he ran an iron chain through the ox's nose and tied it up in a pen. And so men afterwards called the Ox-Herd the Ox-Leader."

When the man heard this, sweat poured over his forehead, and his appetite for joining genealogies was gone.

*— The author's comment (condensed): the wood-carrier was in fact the descendant of an honest official, and the runner in the yamen is the last of a noble line. Yet to go looking for some remote ancestral glory, to think little of one's own forebears, and to pass oneself off as another man's grandson — I do not know what sort of lungs and liver that takes. And if the great ministers rise by favouritism and the foxes all wear official seals, though they are not of one surname and are still proud of the attachment, what wonder is it that men of the same surname should start arguing about who is of the clan?*
