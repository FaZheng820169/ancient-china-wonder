  鎮江包某，年少美丰姿，娶室王氏。包世業賈，常與同事者往來閭巷。乾隆庚子秋日，偕數友為狎邪之遊，日暮乃返。王氏方同一老嫗入廚下治晚餐，聞叩門聲，命老嫗往啟，見一少婦盛妝而入，直赴內室，問之不答。嫗疑為姻戚，往告王氏。王急趨至室，則包在焉，因大笑老嫗目昏，誤認主人為婦人也。忽包作女態斂衽而前，與王氏寒暄，且言：「包郎在某娼家飲酒時，我在門後專守，俟其出，方得同回。」王見其聲音舉動不類包郎，恐其瘋狂，急召僮僕及鄰里姻戚共來看視。包皆一一與見，禮儀周到，稱謂無誤，宛然一大家女也。或男子稍與相狎，鬼即怒曰：「我貞女也，誰近我，我即取其命！」眾問：「你與包有何仇？」鬼曰：「妾與包實因愛成仇，曾控告於城隍神，前後共十九狀，俱未見準。今又告於東嶽帝君，始蒙批准，不日與包同往矣。」詢其姓名，鬼曰：「我好人家兒女，姓名不可聞也。」「告包者何詞？」鬼即連誦十九詞，其詞甚急，不能悉曉，大概控包負心，令彼無歸之意。或又問：「汝即託包身而言，包今何在？」鬼微笑曰：「渠被我縛在城隍廟側小屋中矣。」王氏泣拜，求放其夫，鬼不答。至夜分，眾姻戚私語曰：「彼鬼曾言告城隍狀不準，今縛包於城隍廟側，何不往告於神，求其伸理？」於是共覓香燭楮鏹，若將往者。鬼忽言曰：「今諸人既同來相求，且放彼歸，自有東嶽審斷。」言畢倒地。少頃包蘇，極稱困頓，眾環問所見，包曰：「初出某娼門，即見此婦相隨。初尚或左或右，至教場，婦遽前扯我往城隍廟左側小屋內，黑暗中以繩縛我手足，置之於地，旁似有相守之人。適聞婦來曰：『今且放汝歸。』推我出戶，一跌而醒，身已在家。此事明日東嶽當傳審矣。」再詢其細，包惟酣睡而已。次日午後起曰：「差人至矣，速具酒食。」自出廳向空座拱揖，語多不解。酒既設，復歸臥床上，更許死矣，惟心頭微熱。王氏與諸人泣守之，見包面色時青時紅時黃，變幻不測。三更後，胸前及喉頰間見紅斑爪痕數處。次夜二鼓，髮辮忽散亂。至曉始蘇，索茶飯盡十數器，吞嚥迅速，觀者駭然。少定，呼「取酒食款差役」，王氏如前設之；又命取紙錢六千，須去其破缺者，以四千焚於廳前，二千焚於門側巷內。復自起至大門作拜送狀，反室熟睡兩日乃能起。悉言所見：自女鬼解縛放回後，次日下午，有二差役來傳，其一不識，其一陳姓，亦賈人子，兒時與包為同窗友。陳家貧，娶婦時，包曾助以錢數千文，今已歿三載。謂包曰：「此事已發速報司審辦，爾我同窗好友，在生又承高�誼，自當用情照應，不必上刑具。」同行至中途，見二役鎖前女鬼，鬼大恚，以首觸包，手抓傷包面頰，此包身所以有紅斑爪痕之現也。女鬼詈二差賣法，差不得已，為包亦上鎖同行。路愈遠愈黑，陰風慘烈，辮髮俱散。至一處，彷彿見衙署，差令坐地守候。旋見二紅燈由內出，二差去包鎖，帶入跪於燈止處。見有公案文卷，一官上坐，紅袍烏紗，以手捋須，問曰：「汝包某耶？」包應曰：「諾。」官即提女鬼至，訊答語頗多。女與包並跪階下，相去尺許，絕不聞其一字。見官震怒，令批女鬼頰十五，即上枷鎖，二役牽之，痛哭而去。包初跪案前，覺沮洳泥濘，陰風吹髮，面上絲絲如刀刺，寒慄難當。迨批女頰時，陳役從旁悄言曰：「老兄官司已贏矣，吾為兄辮起髮來。」包再舉首，燈與官俱不復見。二役乃送之回，言明差錢四千文，其二千，則陳役所私得也。人問包：「曾識此女否？」包力言不識。揣其情，女鬼因慕包之色而亡，又欲招包以偕陰耦，逞私妄控，故為陰司所責譴。

*Condensed from the original Chinese account in Zibuyu; the nineteen charges and the longer courtroom exchanges are summarised rather than given in full.*

A Bao of Zhenjiang, young and handsome, married a woman of the Wang family. Bao's family trade was commerce, and he often went among the lanes with his fellows. On an autumn day in the gengzi year of Qianlong he went with several friends on a dissolute excursion and returned at dusk. Wang was in the kitchen with an old nurse preparing the evening meal when they heard a knock; the nurse was sent to open the door, and saw a richly dressed young woman enter and go straight to the inner chamber, and would not answer when asked. The nurse took her for a relative and went to tell Wang, who hurried in — and there was Bao. She laughed at the nurse's dim eyes, mistaking her master for a woman.

Suddenly Bao assumed a woman's manner and, with a curtsey, came forward and exchanged greetings with Wang, saying: "While Bao-lang was drinking at a certain brothel, I kept watch behind the door, and only came back with him when he came out." Wang saw that his voice and bearing were not like Bao's, feared he was mad, and hastily called servants and neighbouring kin to come see. Bao met them all, courteous and exact in address, every inch a great-family daughter. If any man so much as trifled with her, the ghost would fly into a rage: "I am a chaste girl; whoever comes near me, I will take his life!"

They asked: "What enmity have you with Bao?" The ghost said: "The affection between Bao and me turned to enmity; I have filed suit with the City God — nineteen suits in all, none yet admitted. Now I have appealed to the Emperor of the Eastern Peak, and at last it is approved; in a few days I shall go with Bao." Asked her name, the ghost said: "I am a good family's daughter; my name may not be heard." "What do you charge Bao with?" The ghost then recited the nineteen charges in a rush, too quick to follow, the gist being that Bao broke her heart and left her with no home. Someone asked: "You speak through Bao's body — where is Bao now?" The ghost smiled: "He is bound in the little house by the City God's temple." Wang wept and bowed, begging the release of her husband; the ghost gave no answer.

At midnight the kin whispered: "The ghost said the City God would not admit her suit, yet she has bound Bao by the City God's temple — why not go and plead with the god for justice?" So they gathered incense and paper money as if to go. The ghost suddenly said: "Since you have all come to entreat me, I will release him for now; the Eastern Peak will judge in any case." With that she fell to the floor.

Soon Bao revived, utterly spent. They asked what he had seen; Bao said: "When I left the brothel I saw this woman follow. At first now left now right; at the drill ground she suddenly seized me and dragged me to the little house left of the City God's temple, bound my hands and feet in the dark with rope, and laid me on the ground, with someone seemingly standing guard. Then I heard her say, 'I release you for now,' and she pushed me out the door; I fell and woke, and found myself home. Tomorrow the Eastern Peak will summon us for trial." Pressed for more, Bao only slept.

The next afternoon he rose and said: "The bailiffs are come; quickly set out wine and food." He went to the hall and bowed to an empty seat, much of it unintelligible. The wine set out, he went back to bed and at the second watch died, with only the heart faintly warm. Wang and the others wept at his side; they saw Bao's face turn now green now red now yellow, changing without pattern. After the third watch, red marks and claw-traces appeared on his chest and throat and cheeks. The next night at the second drum his queue suddenly came loose. At dawn he revived, called for tea and food, a dozen vessels, swallowing swiftly, to the onlookers' horror. A little settled, he cried "set wine to entertain the bailiffs"; Wang set it out as before; then he called for six thousand paper coins, the broken ones to be removed, and burned four thousand before the hall and two thousand in the lane by the gate. He rose and made a bowing, seeing-off gesture at the great gate, returned and slept two days before he could rise. Then he told all he had seen:

After the girl-ghost released him, the next afternoon two bailiffs came to summon him, one he did not know, the other surnamed Chen, also a merchant's son, who as a boy had been Bao's schoolmate. Chen's family was poor; when he married, Bao had helped with several thousand cash, and he had been dead three years. Chen said to Bao: "This matter has been referred to the Office of Swift Retribution for trial; we were school friends in life and you showed me kindness, so of course I will show favour and need not put on the irons." They went together; halfway they saw two bailiffs locking the girl-ghost, who flew into a rage and butted Bao with her head and clawed his face — which is why Bao's body showed the red marks and claw-traces. The girl cursed the two bailiffs for selling the law; they had no choice and put irons on Bao too. The road grew darker the farther they went, a bitter wind, their queues scattered.

They came to a place that seemed a yamen; the bailiffs bade him sit and wait. Then two red lanterns came out from within; the bailiffs removed Bao's irons and brought him in, kneeling where the lanterns stopped. There was a bench and documents; an official sat above, in red robe and black gauze cap, stroking his beard, and asked: "Are you Bao so-and-so?" Bao answered yes. The official brought up the girl-ghost and questioned her at length. She and Bao knelt side by side at the foot of the steps, a foot apart, and he heard not a single word of it. He saw the official fly into a rage and order fifteen slaps to the girl's cheeks, then put her in cangue and irons, and two bailiffs led her away weeping.

At first Bao knelt before the bench feeling mud and mire, the wind cutting his face like knives, shivering. When the girl's cheeks were slapped, Chen the bailiff whispered from the side: "Elder brother, you have won your suit; let me bind up your queue." Bao looked up again and the lanterns and the official were gone. The two bailiffs escorted him back, settling the bailiff fee at four thousand cash, of which two thousand was Chen's private take.

People asked Bao: "Did you ever know this girl?" Bao insisted he did not. The belief is that the girl-ghost, smitten with Bao's looks, died of love and sought to draw him into a ghostly union, and having falsely accused him was rebuked by the underworld.
